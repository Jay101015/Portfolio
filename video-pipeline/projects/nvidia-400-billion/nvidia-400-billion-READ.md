# Nvidia Lost $400 Billion in 2 Days. Here's What I'm Doing

*Read the plain text. Bold lines are section names, not read aloud. Lines in [BRACKETS] are yours to confirm or change.*

**Opening**

Nvidia just lost more than $400 billion of its value in 2 days. And if you own a tracker fund, some of that money was yours.

Should you worry? A little, but far less than the headlines suggest. On a £10,000 S&P 500 tracker, Nvidia's fall took off about £70. What's behind it is the bigger story: the CEO, an affair he's admitted, and an alleged Chinese spy.

So here's what happened, why it's Nvidia of all companies, what it means for AI and for your investments, and at the end, what I'm doing with my own money.

**What happened**

On Sunday the 8th of November, Nvidia's CEO, Jensen Huang, wrote to every employee. He said he had been unfaithful to his wife. He apologised, and he said he took full responsibility.

The same day, Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation. Not into the affair itself, but into reports that he had been the target of a foreign intelligence operation.

According to those reports, which began in the New York Times, American intelligence agencies believe the woman involved was working for China. She's a businesswoman whose company had reportedly bought billions of dollars of Nvidia's chips. That is an allegation. Nobody has been charged, and Beijing denies it.

The board says it has no reason to believe that company secrets or national security were compromised, and that it has confidence in him. As I record this, he is still the CEO. And a former US prosecutor has been hired to lead the investigation.

The market didn't wait for the answer. On Monday, the shares fell 4.3%. On Tuesday, another 5.5%. That's about 9.5% in 2 days, and roughly $428 billion of value.

**Why Nvidia**

So why would anyone target the boss of a chip company?

Because Nvidia's chips are what artificial intelligence runs on. Most of the big AI systems in the world are trained on them. That makes them one of the most valuable things any country can get hold of.

The US restricts which of those chips can be sold to China. And Jensen Huang has argued publicly for loosening those rules, with direct access to the president. One chip analyst told CNBC that in 35 years, he hadn't seen a chip CEO with this much influence on export policy.

So according to the reporting, the aim wasn't only secrets. It was influence: to shape how the most important man in AI chips thinks about selling to China. Again, that is the claim. The investigation is there to find out what's true.

**What it means**

So what does it mean for AI, and for your money? Here's the first thing to notice. On Monday, while Nvidia fell 4.3%, the Nasdaq, the main US tech index, rose 2.7%. Investors treated this as an Nvidia problem, not an AI problem.

And the business itself hasn't changed. Last quarter, Nvidia's sales were $96.2 billion, more than double a year earlier. The companies buying those chips aren't going to stop because of the CEO's private life.

But there are 2 real risks. The first is the man himself. Analysts say Nvidia has no clear successor. One of them put it like this: Jensen is Nvidia, Nvidia is Jensen. If the investigation forced him out, nobody knows who would run the most valuable company in AI.

The second is politics. The scandal has given new momentum to bills in Congress that would tighten the rules on selling AI chips abroad. So the lasting effect on AI may not come from the boardroom at all. It may come from Washington.

And here's what the headlines skip. Nvidia was already falling. Between its record high on the 6th of October and the Friday before this broke, the shares had dropped 23%, largely on worries about how long the AI spending boom can last. The scandal took 9.5%. The spending worry had already taken 23%.

There are 2 dates to watch. Nvidia is due to report its results on the 17th of November. And members of Congress have asked Jensen Huang for a briefing on the 18th.

What does history say? McDonald's fired its CEO over a relationship with an employee in 2019. A year later, the shares were about 12% higher. Intel's CEO resigned for a similar reason in 2018. A year later, they were about 4% lower. In both cases, what the business did next mattered far more than the scandal.

**What I'm doing**

So here's where I stand. Nvidia is the biggest company in the S&P 500: about 7% of the index. So in an S&P 500 fund, like the one I hold, about £7 of every £100 is in Nvidia.

That's why Nvidia's fall took only about £70 off a £10,000 tracker. If that £10,000 had all been in Nvidia, it would have lost about £950. The other 499 companies did their job.

[I own some Nvidia shares directly as well, and I haven't sold any of them.]

But I want to be honest about the limits of that. The 10 biggest companies now make up about 39% of the S&P 500, and most of them are big tech. Owning 500 companies is not the same as owning 500 equal bets.

So this is how I hedge. [One: alongside my S&P 500 fund, I hold a global fund, so less of my money rides on US tech.] [Two: I keep any single share to a small slice of what I invest, so one company's bad week can't do real damage.] And three: I invest the same amount every month, whatever the headlines say. When prices fall, the same money buys more.

I don't know how this ends for Jensen Huang, and neither does anyone else. That's the point. You don't have to predict it, if no single company can sink you. This is education, not financial advice, and as I said, I own Nvidia shares myself.
