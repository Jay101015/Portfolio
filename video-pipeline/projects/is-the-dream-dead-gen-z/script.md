Since the start of 2021, prices have gone up by about thirty percent. So a hundred pounds today buys what seventy-seven pounds bought then.

[broll search="uk supermarket shopping basket food prices" caption="The weekly shop" cards="red:food +38% since before Covid@thirty-eight" src="08-uk-supermarket-shopping-basket-food-pric.mp4" credit="Video 47601 by Engin_Akyurt via Pixabay"]

[stat value="£1,406" label="Average UK private rent, a month" sub="up almost 4% in a year" source="ONS, October 2026" art=rent-day art_at=tr art_size=0.45 art_start=3]

[broll lib="uk-flats-block-drone" caption="Rent day" cards="red:over a third of our pay@third"]

[stat value="9%" label="Student loan, on pay over £25,000" sub="Plan 5 graduates, England" source="Department for Education, August 2025" cards="ink:our parents never paid it@never; ink:uni in England, recently@uni; red:9% of everything over £25K@everything; red:straight to your student loan@loan"]

[stat value="£1,300 a year" label="Student loan on the typical £39K salary" sub="9% of what you earn over £25K" cards="red:a second tax, for decades@second" source="9% over £25,000: Department for Education, August 2025"]
On the typical salary of thirty-nine thousand three hundred, that's about thirteen hundred pounds a year. A second tax most of us will pay for decades.

[text wrong="Pay kept up with prices" board="Real pay: barely past 2008 | 18 years: prices kept moving | Our pay basically didn't" art=maya-counting tint=red]
And here's the thing that really gets me. Once you take prices into account, average pay is only a few percent higher than it was in 2008. Eighteen years. Prices kept moving. Our pay basically didn't.

That's more than double the typical salary.

[text board="The bigger threat: the jobs" art=maya-worried cards="ink:cost of living: squeezed us@squeezed; red:prices -> the jobs@jobs"]
So the cost of living has squeezed us. But the bigger threat to our generation isn't prices. It's what's happening to the jobs.

[takeaway title="The jobs are changing" n="2" of="3"]
Because the jobs we start our careers in are disappearing fast.

[chart bar title="UK graduate job ads, July" source="Adzuna, July 2026" cards="red:lowest since records began@lowest; red:nearly half the doors, closed@doors"]

In July, there were about 8,400 graduate job adverts in the UK, down from over 15,000 a year earlier. The lowest since records began in 2016. Nearly half the doors, closed in a single year.

[stat value="−30%" label="Entry-level job ads since ChatGPT launched" sub="November 2022 to now" source="Adzuna, 2025"]
Entry-level roles are down by more than thirty percent since ChatGPT launched.

[text board="16 to 24s out of work: 16%" art=job-interview cards="red:highest since 2015@highest"]
And unemployment for sixteen to twenty-four year olds has hit sixteen percent, the highest since 2015.

[broll lib="tower-bridge-office-window" caption="Hiring costs more" cards="red:higher National Insurance@insurance; red:higher minimum wage@minimum"]
Now, it's not all AI. Hiring has got more expensive too, with higher National Insurance and a higher minimum wage.

[broll search="office worker laptop artificial intelligence screen" caption="Tasks, not whole jobs" cards="ink:first drafts@drafts; ink:summarising@summarising; ink:simple questions@questions; ink:sorting data@sorting" src="23-office-worker-laptop-artificial-intellig.mp4" credit="Video 197 by Life-Of-Vids via Pixabay"]
But AI is doing the exact tasks junior people used to learn on. Writing first drafts. Summarising documents. Answering simple customer questions. Sorting data. That's how most of us start a career, and that's what's going first.

[broll lib="businessman-phone-city" caption="The boss's side" cards="red:why hire 3 juniors?@juniors; ink:1 person + AI@person; ink:the hiring just shrinks@shrinks"]
Think about it from the boss's side. Why hire three juniors to write first drafts when one person with AI can do it in an afternoon? The hiring doesn't stop. It just shrinks.

[set room=office cards="ink:me: AI consultant for law firms@consulting; ink:sorting documents@sorting; ink:drafting statements@drafting"]
{desk-type} And I see this up close, because it's {desk-point} literally my job. I work in AI consulting, {desk-type} helping law firms use AI properly. Every week, I watch tasks that used to take a person {desk-shocked} hours get done, such as {desk-reading} sorting through a document and drafting up a statement. 

[text board="Left behind: the ones who won't learn it | Use it well: harder to replace" art=rabbit art_at=tr art_size=0.32 art_start=1 cards="red:AI replaces you -> won't learn it@refuse; green:faster@faster; green:more valuable@valuable"]
But here's what nobody tells you. The people getting left behind aren't the ones AI replaces. It's the ones who refuse to learn it. The people who use it well become faster, more valuable and harder to replace.

[broll search="nurse talking with patient hospital" caption="People" cards="ink:nursing · teaching@nursing; ink:selling · leading a team@leading; green:people@people" src="27-nurse-talking-with-patient-hospital.mp4" credit="Video 143376 by Amorn_mimi via Pixabay"]
So which jobs are safest? The ones built on three things AI is still bad at. People: nursing, teaching, selling, leading a team.

[broll lib="tradesman-van-tools" focus="50 45" caption="Hands" cards="green:hands@hands"]
Hands: electricians, plumbers, anything physical in a real building.

[broll lib="drill-in-hand" caption="AI can't do this" cards="red:AI can't rewire your kitchen@rewire"]
AI can draft a contract, but it can't rewire your kitchen.

[broll search="hand signing document with pen close up" caption="Judgement" pick=2 cards="green:judgement@judgement; ink:signs off@signs; red:takes the blame@blame" src="30-hand-signing-document-with-pen-close-up.mp4" credit="Video 131993 by Dx21 via Pixabay"]
And judgement: the person who signs off, owns the decision, and takes the blame if it goes wrong.

[text board="AI is creating jobs too" art=plant art_at=tr art_size=0.32 art_start=0.3 cards="green:new jobs, made by AI@creating; green:roles like mine@mine; ink:barely existed a few years ago@existed"]
And AI is creating jobs too. Roles like mine, helping firms use it properly, barely existed a few years ago.

First, the money. Our generation has one huge advantage our parents didn't: access. In the 90s, investing meant a broker, big fees and a lump sum. Today, you can open a stocks and shares ISA on your phone in about ten minutes and start with a few pounds.

[chart line title="£200 a month at 7%: start at 22 or at 32" y="£" x="Age" source="7% a year, paid monthly, illustrative" cards="ink:£200 a month · 7% a year@hundred; green:start at 22: £450K@fifty; red:start at 32: £208K@thirty-two"]

And time does the heavy lifting. Two hundred pounds a month from age twenty-two, growing at about seven percent a year, could be worth around four hundred and fifty grand by the time you're sixty. Start at about thirty-two, and it's about two hundred and eight thousand.

[news search="FTSE 100 biggest one day fall" board="Markets fall | Some years, a lot" art=phone-balance-drop cards="red:your pot goes down@down; ink:the price of the growth@price"]

[broll src="jeffrey-index-holdings.mp4" phone=on vol=0 caption="Hundreds of companies" credit="The Everyday Investor" cards="ink:one fund, hundreds of companies@hundreds; green:bad years = buying cheaper@cheaper; red:panic and sell = get hurt@panic"]
An index fund spreads your money across hundreds of companies, and if you keep paying in through the bad years, you're buying cheaper. The people who get hurt are usually the ones who panic and sell.

[text board="3 months of essentials first" art=umbrella art_at=tr art_size=0.4 art_start=0.3 cards="ink:easy-access savings@easy-access; red:a broken boiler@boiler; red:never sell at the bottom@bottom"]
That's also why I'd keep three months of essentials in easy-access savings first, so a broken boiler never forces you to sell at the bottom.

[stat value="£1,000" label="Lifetime ISA bonus, at most, a year" sub="25% bonus on what you save" source="GOV.UK, 2026" cards="green:+25% on what you save@bonus; ink:up to £1,000 a year@thousand"]
And if a home is the goal, a Lifetime ISA adds a twenty-five percent bonus to what you save, up to a thousand pounds a year,

[broll src="jeffrey-ai-everyday.mp4" phone=on from=7 vol=0 caption="My plan" credit="The Everyday Investor" cards="green:get good at AI@good; green:1: use AI every day@one; ink:for real work, not for fun@real"]
And right now, the fastest way to raise your pay is to get good at AI. Here's what I'd do. One: use AI every day, for real work, not for fun.

[text board="2: ask it well | Context · an example | What good looks like" art=maya-surprised cards="red:check its work@check; red:it hallucinates@hallucinate"]
Two: learn to ask it well. Give it the context, show it an example, tell it what good looks like. And check its work, because AI does get things wrong very confidently. It's known to hallucinate.

[broll caption="3: what AI can't do" cards="green:ownership@ownership; green:decisions@decisions; red:AI can't sit across the table@sit" lib="office-interview-handshake" focus="50 40"]
