Half of everyone who pays income tax in the UK earns less than £29,700 a year.

To get into the top 10%, you need more than double that. And to be in the top 10% for wealth, the bar is over a million pounds.

So where are you? I'll show you every line on the ladder, the line for your age, the tax traps waiting on the way up, and at the end, the number that actually decides whether you're rich. And it's not your salary.

[takeaway title="The middle" n="1" of="5"]
Let's start in the middle, because most people guess it wrong.

[broll search="busy london street crowd of people walking" caption="9 in 10" cards="ink:9 in 10@9; ink:the middle: half above, half below@half"]
Quickly, what these lines mean. Being in the top 10% means you earn more than 9 out of every 10 taxpayers. The top 1% means you earn more than 99 out of 100. And the middle is just that: half the country above you, half below.

[broll search="london commuters walking to work morning" caption="36.7 million taxpayers" cards="ink:36.7 million people@36.7"]
In the last tax year with full figures, 36.7 million people in the UK paid income tax. Imagine lining every one of them up, from the lowest income to the highest, and walking to the person standing exactly in the middle.

[stat value="£29,700" label="The middle UK taxpayer, before tax" sub="half earn less, half earn more" source="HMRC Personal Incomes Statistics 2023 to 2024, April 2026" art=sam-thinking art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:before tax@before"]
That person earns £29,700 a year, before tax. Half of all taxpayers earn less than that. Half earn more.

[stat value="£2,075" label="What £29,700 lands as, a month" sub="after income tax and National Insurance" source="Worked out at 2026-27 rates, England; no pension or student loan" art=payday art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:a month@month"]
After income tax and National Insurance, that's about £2,075 a month landing in your account. If that's roughly you, you're not behind. You're exactly in the middle of the country.

[text board="Before tax vs what lands" art=calculator-table art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:before tax@before; green:after tax@lands"]
And that's worth saying clearly, because it trips a lot of people up. Every figure in this video is before tax unless I say otherwise. What lands in your account is always less, and that's the number you actually live on.

[broll search="person scrolling social media on phone" caption="Your feed is not the middle" cards="ink:your feed@feed; ink:London@London"]
It probably doesn't feel like the middle, and there's a reason. Your feed is full of people at the top, and if you live in London, everything around you is priced for people earning far more than the middle.

[stat value="68%" count=off label="Average UK rent, as a share of the middle take-home" sub="£1,406 rent from £2,075 a month" source="ONS private rents, October 2026; take-home worked out at 2026-27 rates" art=rent-day art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:£1,406 a month rent@£1,406; red:68% of the middle take-home@68%"]
Here's why the middle feels so tight. The average private rent in the UK is now about £1,406 a month. On the middle take-home of £2,075, that's about 68% of your pay gone before you've bought a single thing. And that's the UK average, not London.

[broll lib="tower-bridge-office-window" caption="Priced for the top" cards="red:£111,000 for London's average home@£111,000"]
To put a number on it: to buy the average home in London, you'd need to earn around £111,000 a year. I worked that out in a whole video, and it's on the channel.

[list title="Three salaries, where they sit" art=sam-counting art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:£25,000: just under the middle@£25,000; ink:£45,000: well above it@£45,000; green:£70,000: top 10%@£70,000"]
- £25,000: about £1,793 a month
- £45,000: about £2,993 a month
- £70,000: about £4,263 a month
Let's make it concrete. On £25,000, you take home about £1,793 a month, and you're just under the middle. On £45,000, about £2,993 a month, and you're well above it, but still a long way from the top 10%. On £70,000, about £4,263 a month, and you've just crossed into the top 10%. Notice how big the gap is between £45,000 and £67,400. That's the climb most people are actually on.

[text board="These are taxpayers, not everyone" art=look-closer art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:only taxpayers@only; ink:all taxable income@taxable"]
One honest thing before we climb. These figures only count people who pay income tax, and they include all taxable income, not just salary. People who earn too little to pay tax aren't in them at all. So if you're in the top 10% of taxpayers, you're even higher among all adults.

[takeaway title="The top 10%" n="2" of="5"]
Now the line in the title.

[list title="Climbing the ladder" art=staircase-coins art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:the middle@middle; green:top 10%@top"]
- The middle: £29,700
- The top 10%: £67,400
- Take-home: £4,137 a month
So, how far is the climb from the middle to the top 10%? To be in the top 10% of UK taxpayers, you need an income of more than £67,400 a year. After tax and National Insurance, that's about £4,137 a month.

[stat value="£67,400" label="The top 10% of UK taxpayers starts here" sub="before tax" source="HMRC Personal Incomes Statistics 2023 to 2024, April 2026" art=sam-surprised art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:1 in 10@1; ink:more than double the middle@double"]
That's 1 in 10 taxpayers, and it's more than double the middle. If you earn that, you are, on paper, in the top 10% of the country.

[broll lib="businessman-phone-city" caption="Top 10% on paper" cards="ink:team leaders, senior nurses, engineers@team"]
And it's not a banker's salary. A team leader, a senior nurse, an experienced engineer, a good tradesperson with their own van. Plenty of normal jobs get there by their thirties or forties.

[stat value="£3,657" label="A top 10% earner's real take-home, a month" sub="with a 5% pension and a Plan 5 student loan" source="Worked out at 2026-27 rates, England; student loan 9% over £25,000 (Department for Education)" art=student-loan-letter art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:5% into a pension@pension; red:£318 a month to the student loan@£318"]
But that £4,137 is the best case. Put 5% into a workplace pension, and if you went to uni recently, add a student loan at 9% of everything over £25,000. That's about £318 a month on its own. Now the top 10% earner takes home about £3,657.

[broll search="parent dropping child at nursery morning" caption="Top 10% doesn't feel rich" cards="red:rent or a mortgage@mortgage; red:childcare@childcare"]
Which is why a lot of people in the top 10% don't feel rich at all. £3,657 a month sounds like a lot, until rent or a mortgage, childcare and a car come out of it.

[stat value="38%" count=off label="Average UK rent, as a share of a top 10% take-home" sub="£1,406 from £3,657 a month" source="ONS private rents, October 2026; take-home worked out at 2026-27 rates" art=sam-thinking art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:38% instead of 68%@38%"]
To be fair, it's a lot better than the middle. The same average rent is about 38% of that top 10% take-home, instead of 68%. That gap is real. It just doesn't feel like luxury, it feels like breathing room. And remember the wealth number I mentioned at the start. That's coming, and it's the one that changes how you see all of this.

[text board="Above £50,270: 42p of every £1 goes" art=tax-return art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="red:40% income tax@40%; ink:plus 2% National Insurance@2%"]
And the climb gets more expensive as you go. From about £50,270, every extra pound you earn is taxed at 40%, plus 2% National Insurance.

[stat value="£580" label="What you keep of a £1,000 pay rise, above £50,270" sub="you never take home less after a rise" source="2026-27 income tax and National Insurance rates, England" art=pay-rise-ask art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="green:£580 of every £1,000@£580; ink:only the part above the line@only"]
So of a £1,000 pay rise up there, you keep about £580. But here's a myth worth killing. Going into a higher band never means you take home less overall. The 40% only applies to the part of your pay above the line, not to all of it.

[text board="£12,570 tax-free, frozen since 2021" art=frozen art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:£12,570@£12,570; red:frozen since 2021@2021; red:more people in the 40% band@band"]
And the lines themselves haven't moved. The tax-free Personal Allowance has been stuck at £12,570 since 2021, and the 40% line hasn't moved either. So every time pay goes up with inflation, more people get pulled into the higher band without feeling any richer. If you've had a pay rise and it feels like less of it reaches you, that's why.

[bite value="£2,250" lose="£2,250" pct=100 label="Child Benefit for two children, a year, all of it gone by £80,000" sub="it is clawed back between £60,000 and £80,000" source="GOV.UK Child Benefit rates 2025-26 and High Income Child Benefit Charge" cards="red:from £60,000@£60,000; red:all of it by £80,000@£80,000"]
There's a second trap if you have kids. From £60,000, the Child Benefit you get starts being taken back through your tax bill, and by £80,000 you lose all of it. Right around the top 10% line.

[broll search="edinburgh old town street" caption="Scotland: different bands" cards="ink:Scottish rates@Scotland"]
And a quick note if you're in Scotland: income tax bands are different there, so your take-home on the same salary will be a bit different. The ladder itself is the same.

[takeaway title="The top 1%" n="3" of="5"]
Now keep climbing. Because the people you probably think of as rich are a lot higher up.

[stat value="£93,600" count=off label="Around where the top 5% starts" sub="before tax" source="HMRC Personal Incomes Statistics 2023 to 2024, April 2026" art=sam-counting art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:1 in 20@20"]
The top 5% starts at around £93,600. That's 1 in 20 taxpayers.

[stat value="£207,000" label="The top 1% of UK taxpayers" sub="about £134,000 after income tax" source="HMRC Personal Incomes Statistics 2023 to 2024, April 2026" art=sam-celebrating art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:£134,000 after income tax@£134,000; green:1 in 100@100"]
And the top 1% starts at £207,000 a year. After income tax, that's about £134,000. 1 in 100.

[text board="The top 1% line moved £6,000 in a year" art=chart-up art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:£201,000@£201,000; green:£207,000@£207,000"]
And that line moves. In just one year it rose from £201,000 to £207,000. The top keeps pulling away, which is another reason chasing a salary number alone is a tiring way to measure yourself.

[broll lib="luxury-cars-parked" caption="The top 1%" cards="ink:seven times the middle@seven"]
That's about seven times the middle. And this is where most of the people you think of as rich actually sit. Senior doctors, partners in firms, company directors, people who own businesses.

[bite value="£1" lose="60p" label="Every £1 you earn between £100,000 and £125,140" sub="plus 2% National Insurance on top" source="GOV.UK income tax rates and Personal Allowances, 2026-27" cards="red:60% income tax@60%; red:62% with National Insurance@62%"]
But on the way there, there's the biggest trap of the lot. Once your income goes over £100,000, you start losing your tax-free Personal Allowance: £1 of it for every £2 you earn. So on every pound between £100,000 and £125,140, you effectively pay 60% income tax. Add National Insurance, and it's 62%.

[stat value="£9,553" label="What you keep of the £25,140 between £100,000 and £125,140" sub="38p in every pound" source="Worked out at 2026-27 rates, England" art=sam-facepalm art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:38p in every pound@38p"]
Put that in money. Earn the full £25,140 between £100,000 and £125,140, and you keep about £9,553 of it. 38p in every pound.

[text board="The way around it: your pension" art=pension-letter art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:pension contributions@pension; green:into your future@future"]
That's why some people near that line put the extra into their pension instead. Pension contributions can bring your taxable income back under £100,000, so the money goes into your future instead of the 60% band. If you're anywhere near that line, it's worth getting proper advice before you cross it.

[takeaway title="The line for your age" n="4" of="5"]
Now, the line that's actually fair to compare yourself against. Your age.

[text board="At 25, top 10% isn't the same as at 45" art=calendar art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:full-time employees@full-time"]
Comparing a 25-year-old with a 45-year-old makes no sense. Pay climbs with experience. So here's the top 10% line for full-time employees, by age.

[list title="Top 10% full-time pay, by age" anim=bar-race art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:22 to 29@22; ink:30 to 39@30; green:40 to 49: the peak@peaks; ink:50 to 59@50"]
- 22 to 29: £52,200
- 30 to 39: £75,000
- 40 to 49: £88,700
- 50 to 59: £85,200
From 22 to 29, you're in the top 10% from about £52,200. From 30 to 39, about £75,000. From 40 to 49, it peaks at about £88,700. And from 50 to 59, it drops slightly, to about £85,200.

[stat value="£3,403" label="Top 10% at 22 to 29, a month after tax" sub="on £52,200, no pension or student loan" source="ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings 2025, provisional; 2026-27 rates" art=graduate art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:top 10% for your age@age"]
So if you're in your twenties on £52,000, you're doing better than 9 in 10 people your age, full-time. That's about £3,403 a month after tax. Even if it doesn't feel like it next to someone fifteen years older.

[broll lib="uni-students-library" caption="It's a long game" cards="ink:the peak comes in your forties@forties"]
And notice the peak. For most people, earnings top out in their forties, then level off. Which means the habits you build in your twenties and thirties matter far more than the salary you're on right now.

[list title="Top 10% by age, a month after tax" art=calendar art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:£4,505 in your thirties@£4,505; green:£5,167 in your forties@£5,167"]
- 22 to 29: about £3,403
- 30 to 39: about £4,505
- 40 to 49: about £5,167
- 50 to 59: about £4,998
In take-home terms, that's about £3,403 a month in your twenties, £4,505 in your thirties, £5,167 at the peak in your forties, and just under £5,000 in your fifties. Before pensions and student loans.

[broll lib="office-interview-handshake" caption="Pay climbs with experience" cards="green:£52,200 to £88,700@£88,700"]
The top 10% line goes from about £52,200 in your twenties to about £88,700 in your forties. That's the job market paying for experience. Your salary will very likely grow. The real question is what you do with it as it does.

[text board="Top 10% income ≠ top 10% rich" art=house-scales art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:what they keep@keep"]
Because here's the twist. Earning in the top 10% does not make you rich. Two people on £67,400 can end up in completely different places, depending on one thing: how much of it they keep.

[takeaway title="The number that really counts" n="5" of="5"]
And that brings us to the number I promised at the start. The one that's not your salary.

[stat value="£293,700" label="The middle household's total wealth, Great Britain" sub="£181,700 without pensions" source="ONS Wealth and Assets Survey, April 2020 to March 2022" art=piggy-coin art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:your home, your pension, your savings@home; ink:£181,700 without pensions@£181,700"]
Wealth is everything you own, minus everything you owe: your home, your pension, your savings and investments. The middle household in Great Britain has about £293,700. Take pensions out, and it's about £181,700.

[stat value="£1.2 million" count=off label="The top 10% of households for wealth" sub="the top 1%: about £3.1 million" source="ONS Wealth and Assets Survey, April 2020 to March 2022" art=sam-surprised art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:£1.2 million@£1.2; ink:top 1%: about £3.1 million@£3.1"]
To be in the top 10% of households for wealth, you need about £1.2 million. For the top 1%, about £3.1 million.

[broll lib="uk-semis-street-pan" caption="Most wealth is homes and pensions" cards="ink:homes@Homes; ink:pensions@pensions; green:decades@decades"]
And look at what that wealth mostly is. Homes and pensions. Things that grow slowly, over years. Most households in the top 10% for wealth didn't get there with one big salary. They got there with decades.

[text board="Only what you own counts" art=house-price-tag art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:the value of your home@value; red:minus the mortgage@minus; ink:your pension@pension"]
And it's worth being clear about how wealth is counted. If you own a home, it's the value of the home minus what's left on the mortgage. Your pension counts too, even though you can't touch it yet. Which is why a lot of households that never felt rich, people in their fifties and sixties who've paid off most of a mortgage and paid into a pension for thirty years, end up near the top for wealth. Wealth tends to peak just before retirement.

[text board="High pay, no wealth. Normal pay, real wealth." art=crossroads art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:top 10% income, nowhere near top 10% wealth@nowhere; green:a slice of every payslip, invested@invests"]
So you can be top 10% by income and nowhere near top 10% by wealth. And someone on a normal salary who keeps a slice of every payslip and invests it can end up wealthier than someone who earned twice as much and spent it all.

[chart line title="£300 a month at 7% a year" y="£" x="years" draw=6 source="Illustration: 7% a year average growth, monthly, before fees; not a promise" cards="ink:£300 a month@£300; ink:£108,000 paid in@£108,000; green:worth about £366,000@£366,000"]

Here's what that looks like. £300 a month, invested, growing at 7% a year on average. After 30 years, you've paid in £108,000. But the pot is worth about £366,000. That's the snowball. Most of the money at the end is growth, not what you put in.

[stat value="£540,000" count=off label="£300 a month for 35 years, against 25 years" sub="£243,000 if you start ten years later" source="Illustration: 7% a year average growth, monthly, before fees; not a promise" art=early-bird art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:about £540,000@£540,000; red:about £243,000@£243,000"]
And the single biggest thing in that chart is time. Same £300 a month, same 7%. Start at 25 and invest for 35 years, and you end up with about £540,000. Start ten years later, at 35, and it's about £243,000. Less than half, for only ten fewer years. Those first ten years do more work than the last twenty-five.

[stat value="£1,000 a month" count=off label="for 30 years at 7%: about £1.2 million" sub="the top 10% wealth line, from saving alone" source="Illustration: 7% a year average growth, monthly, before fees and inflation; not a promise" art=snowball art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:about £1.2 million@£1.2; ink:before inflation@inflation"]
And the top 10% wealth line? £1,000 a month, for 30 years, at the same 7%, gets you to about £1.2 million. That's a lot to put away, and inflation means a million will be worth less by then. But it shows the point: the top 10% for wealth is reachable from a good salary and time, without a lottery win.

[broll lib="save-money-coins-graphic" caption="First a cushion, then invest" cards="ink:a few months of spending@months; green:then invest@invest"]
One thing before any of that. Have a cushion first: a few months of spending in an easy-access savings account, so a broken boiler doesn't end up on a credit card. Then invest what you won't need for at least five years.

[broll lib="vuag-holdings-app" phone=on caption="What an index fund owns" marks="ink:circle 50,38@hundreds" cards="green:hundreds of companies@hundreds"]
And it doesn't need to be clever. An index fund like this one holds hundreds of the biggest companies in one go. You don't pick winners. You just own all of them.

[list title="How you actually climb" anim=isa-shield art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="green:£20,000 a year, tax-free@£20,000; green:the employer match@match; ink:time@time"]

- A Stocks and Shares ISA: £20,000 a year, tax-free

So how do you actually climb? Four things. Grow your income: ask for the pay rise, switch jobs, learn the skill. Use a Stocks and Shares ISA, where you can put in up to £20,000 a year and never pay tax on the growth. Take the full employer match on your workplace pension, because that's money you only get if you put yours in. And give it time.

[text board="Grow the number you start from" art=pay-rise-ask art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:ask for the rise@ask; green:switch when you've outgrown it@switch; green:the skill people pay for@skill"]
Start with income, because it's the engine for everything else. Ask for the pay rise, with evidence of what you've delivered, not just how long you've been there. If you've outgrown your job and your employer won't move, switch, because for a lot of people the biggest jumps come from moving. And learn the skill people in your field pay more for. Every pound of pay rise is a pound you can split between living better now and investing for later.

[stat value="3%" count=off label="The least your employer must add to your workplace pension" sub="8% in total, on pay between £6,240 and £50,270" source="GOV.UK workplace pensions, automatic enrolment" art=pension-match art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:at least 3% from your employer@employer; ink:on your qualifying pay@qualifying"]
That employer match is bigger than people think. By law, if you're enrolled in a workplace pension, your employer has to put in at least 3% of your qualifying pay, as long as you put in yours. Some put in far more. If you've opted out, you've turned down part of your pay.

[text board="Under 40? The Lifetime ISA adds 25%" art=lisa-bonus art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="green:25% on top@25%; ink:up to £4,000 a year@£4,000"]
And if you're under 40 and saving for a first home or for later life, a Lifetime ISA adds a 25% government bonus on up to £4,000 a year. That's up to £1,000 a year, free. There are rules on when you can take it out, so read them first.

[text board="Income gets you on the ladder. Keeping it gets you up." art=staircase-coins art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:income@Income; green:keeping it@Keeping"]
Income gets you on the ladder. Keeping it, and investing it, is what gets you up it.

[broll lib="vuag-max-chart-app" phone=on caption="An index fund, left alone" cards="ink:no stock picking@picking; green:just years@years"]
No stock picking, no timing the market. Just money going in every month, and years doing the heavy lifting.
