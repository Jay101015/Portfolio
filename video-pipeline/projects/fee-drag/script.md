Welcome back. Today, one number. {point} One percent. It is the fee on a typical managed fund, or a typical adviser, and it sounds like nothing. {smug} By the end of this video you will see why it is the most expensive small number in your life.

[stat value="1%" label="A typical fund or adviser fee" sub="Charged every year, on everything you have, whether it goes up or down" source="Illustrative" pose=crossed]
Here is how a one percent fee works. It is not one percent of your gains. {point} It is one percent of everything you have invested, taken every single year, in good years and in bad ones. And it never shows up as a bill.

[still src="statement.jpg" credit="Illustration" caption="Where it hides" want="A fund statement with the expense ratio in tiny print" pose=think]
It hides in a line called the expense ratio, buried in a document nobody reads. {think} Your balance just grows a little slower than it should, and you never see the money leave. That is what makes it dangerous.

Take ten thousand dollars and leave it for forty years at seven percent a year. With no fee it grows to about one hundred and fifty thousand. {surprised} With a one percent fee, about one hundred and three thousand. With two percent, about seventy thousand. Same market. Same money. The only difference is the fee.

[stat value="$46,888" from="$0" label="Lost to a 1% fee" sub="On $10,000 over 40 years. Thirty one percent of what you would have had." source="Compound growth at 7% versus 6%, illustrative" count="3" pose=shocked]
That one percent cost you forty seven thousand dollars. {shocked} Nearly a third of the whole pile. Not because the fund did badly. Because a small slice was taken every year, and then that slice could not compound for you.

[compare title="Same market, two funds"]
left: Index fund | 0.03% | Tracks the whole market; About three dollars a year per ten thousand
right: Active fund | 1.0% | Pays managers to beat it; About a hundred dollars a year per ten thousand
So what does the expensive fund give you for the money? {think} An index fund tracks the whole market for three hundredths of a percent. An active fund pays people to try to beat the market, and charges around one percent for the attempt.

[chart bar title="Share of US large-cap funds that beat the S&P 500" y="%" source="S&P Dow Jones Indices, SPIVA US scorecard, year-end 2023"]
bars: Over 1 year:40, Over 5 years:21, Over 15 years:12
And how often does the attempt work? {point} Over one year, about four in ten beat the index. Over five years, about two in ten. {facepalm} Over fifteen years, roughly one in ten. You are paying thirty times the fee for a bet that mostly loses.

[still src="bucket.jpg" credit="Illustration" caption="The leak" want="A bucket of coins with a small hole, coins trickling out" pose=think]
Think of your money as a bucket you keep filling. {think} The market is the tap. A fee is a hole in the bottom. A one percent hole looks tiny, but it is open for forty years, and the water that leaks out never gets to fill the bucket.

[list title="Find your fees in five minutes"]
- Look up the expense ratio of every fund you own
- Ask an adviser what you pay, in dollars, each year
- Compare it with a broad index fund at 0.1% or less
- If the gap buys you nothing you can name, move
{point} Here is the homework. Look up the expense ratio of every fund you own. If you have an adviser, ask what you pay, in dollars, per year. {thumbsup} Compare that with a broad index fund at a tenth of a percent or less. And if the gap is not buying you anything you can actually name, move the money.
