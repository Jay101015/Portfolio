To retire with a million pounds, starting at 25, you need about £329 a month going in. And here's the bit nobody tells you: about £123 of that doesn't come out of your pocket.

[text board="A million sounds like a lottery number" art=off]
And I know a million sounds like a lottery number. It did to me. But when you actually break it down, it's a monthly direct debit and a lot of patience. And most of it isn't even your money.

So I want to show you who pays the rest, what happens to that number if you're starting at 35 or 45, and the one thing that quietly takes a quarter of a million off the end. And then I'll show you where I actually am with mine.

[chart bar title="A month, to reach £1 million by 67" y="£" source="Worked out at 7% a year, paid in monthly; not a promise" part="The number"]
- 25: 329

Right, the number first. I've assumed your money grows about 7% a year, which is roughly what a global tracker has done over the long run. It's not a promise, some years are horrible. Start at 25 and it's £329 a month. Start at 35 and it's £700. Start at 45 and it's about sixteen hundred pounds a month.

[text board="Wait 10 years: the bill doubles" art=off anim=hourglass anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.45]
So every ten years you wait, the monthly bill more than doubles. That's the whole game, honestly. It's not about being clever, it's about being early.

[stat value="£166,000" label="What you actually pay in, from 25" sub="the other £834,000 is growth" source="£329 a month for 42 years, at 7% a year; not a promise"]
And look at this. From 25, over the whole 42 years, what actually goes in is about 166 grand. The other 834 grand is just growth. The money made more money than you did.

[compare title="£329 a month: how long each £100,000 takes" good=right source="Worked out at 7% a year, paid in monthly; not a promise"]
- The first £100,000: nearly 15 years
- The last £100,000: about 17 months
And this is the bit that makes people quit. The first hundred grand takes nearly 15 years. It feels like nothing's happening. But the last hundred grand, from 900 to a million, takes about 17 months. Same money going in. So if you're a few years in and it feels slow, that's normal. That's what it's meant to feel like.

[stat value="£40,000" label="A year, from a £1 million pot" sub="taking about 4% a year" source="The 4% rule of thumb; not a guarantee"]
And what does a million actually pay you? The rule of thumb is you can take about 4% a year. So that's around 40 grand a year, and the pot should roughly last. I did a whole video on whether that's enough, and I'll come back to the catch in a minute.

[text board="So where's the cheap bit?" art=off part="Who pays"]
But 329 quid a month is still a lot when you're 25. So here's where it gets cheaper.

[compare title="£329 a month, on a £35,000 salary" good=left source="Auto-enrolment minimums on qualifying earnings and basic-rate tax relief, GOV.UK, 2026-27"]
- You pay: £206
- Your employer and the taxman: £123
Say you're on 35 grand. Your workplace pension already puts in around £192 a month. But only about £96 of that is yours. Your employer puts in about £72, and the taxman adds about £24. Then you top up the gap yourself, and the taxman adds to that as well. So for £329 going in, about £206 comes out of your pay. The other £123 is free money.

[text board="Employer money first. Always" art=off anim=piggy-bank anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.45]
So the cheapest way, by a mile, starts with the pension at work. If your employer matches more when you put in more, take every pound of it before you do anything else. Nothing else you can buy gives you that.

[stat value="£223" label="A month to start, at 25, if you raise it 3% a year" sub="instead of £329 flat" source="Worked out at 7% a year, contributions rising 3% a year to 67; not a promise"]
And if 329 feels like too much right now, there's another way to do it. Start lower, and put it up a bit every year. If you raise it by 3% a year, basically every time you get a pay rise, you can start at about £223 a month and still get to the same place.

[text board="Raise it when your pay goes up | You won't miss it" art=off anim=arrow-up anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.45]
That's the trick I actually like. You never see the money, so you never miss it. Most workplace pensions let you change the percentage in about two minutes.

[text board="After the pension: a stocks and shares ISA | Up to £20,000 a year" art=off anim=stack-grow anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.42 art_start=0]
After that, it's a stocks and shares ISA. You don't get anything on the way in, but there's no tax at all on the way out, and you can get at it whenever you need to. You can put in up to 20 grand a year, which is more than most of us will ever manage.

[text board="One fund. The whole market. Leave it" art=off]
And what goes inside it? For me it's boring. One cheap tracker fund that buys the whole market. I'm not picking winners, I tried that, and I'll tell you how that went.

[text board="Starting at 35 or 45?" art=off part="Starting later"]
But what if you're not 25. What if you're 35, or 45, and you're looking at that chart thinking, well, that's me done.

[compare title="Starting at 45, by 67" good=right source="Worked out at 7% a year, paid in monthly; not a promise"]
- £1 million: £1,601 a month
- £500,000: £800 a month
It's not done. At 45, a million needs about sixteen hundred a month, which is a lot. But half a million needs about £800. And if your employer's paying in, a good chunk of that isn't yours either.

[stat value="£241.30" label="The full new State Pension, a week" sub="about £12,500 a year, on top" source="GOV.UK, full new State Pension, 2026-27"]
And you're not starting from nothing. The full State Pension is £241.30 a week right now. That's about twelve and a half grand a year on top of whatever you've built, as long as you've got enough years of National Insurance.

[text board="Half a million + the State Pension | Still a good retirement" art=off anim=plant-grow anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.45]
So half a million, plus the State Pension, is still a really comfortable retirement. Starting late doesn't mean you've failed. It just means the number's different.

[text board="What quietly ruins it" art=off part="What ruins it"]
Now the bit that ruins it. And it's not a crash.

[compare title="Same £329 a month, 42 years" good=left source="7% a year against 6% a year after a 1% fee; not a promise"]
- Low fee: £1,000,000
- 1% a year in fees: £746,000
It's fees. Same money in, same 42 years. But if you're paying 1% a year more in fees, you don't end up with a million. You end up with about 746 grand. That's a quarter of a million pounds, gone, for a number most people never look at.

[text board="Check the fee on your pension | It's one login" art=off anim=scales anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.45]
So if you do one thing after this video, log in to your pension and find the fee. A lot of old workplace pensions are sat in expensive funds and nobody's ever checked.

[text board="The other one: selling when it drops" art=off]
The other thing that ruins it is you. Well, me. The market will drop, probably by a lot, probably more than once in 40 years. The 7% only works if you're still in when it comes back.

[text board="Don't look at it every day" art=off anim=calendar-flip anim_labels=off art_at=r art_size=0.42 art_start=0]
What helps me is not looking. I check mine about once a month now. When I was checking it every day, every red day felt like an emergency, and that's when you do something stupid.

[list title="So, the cheap way" unnumbered]
- Employer money first
- Then an ISA, in one cheap tracker
- Check the fee
- Put it up when your pay goes up
- Don't sell when it drops
So that's the whole thing, really. Take the employer money first. Then an ISA, in one cheap tracker. Check the fee. Put it up when your pay goes up. And don't sell when it drops. None of it's clever. It's just hard to keep doing for 40 years.

[stat value="£435,000" label="What £1 million buys in 42 years, in today's money" sub="still a lot. Not a yacht" source="£1,000,000 at 2% inflation a year for 42 years"]
And I've got to be honest about one more thing. A million in 42 years isn't a million today. With prices going up about 2% a year, it buys what roughly 435 grand buys now. That's still a really good retirement. It's just not a yacht.
