**Hook**

If you had bought last year's hottest stock every single January since 2000, your £1,000 would have been worth about £30 by 2011.

The same £1,000 in the S&P 500, left completely alone, is about £9,000 today. It never once dropped below £750.

So I ran the whole race, year by year. I'll show you every year's winner and what it did next, the one year the hype paid off spectacularly, and at the end, the number from the biggest study ever done on this. It settles the argument.

**The rules of the race**

First, the rules, because the rules are the whole point.

Pot one is the boring pot. £1,000 into an S&P 500 index fund in January 2000, dividends reinvested, never touched again.

Pot two is the hype pot. Every January it buys the stock that was the best performer in the S&P 500 the year before,

the one in every headline, and holds it for 12 months. Then it sells and buys the new winner.

Why last year's winner and not this year's? Because nobody knows this year's winner in January. If you somehow could, your £1,000 would be a number with 15 zeros on it, more than all the money on Earth. That is the maths telling you it cannot be done.

**The lost decade**

Now the race. And the first 12 years are brutal.

In 2000 the hottest stock was an energy trader called Dynegy, up more than 200%. The hype pot bought it in January 2001, and it fell 54%. In 2001 the winner was Nvidia, up 308%. The hype pot bought that, and in 2002 it lost 83%. Two years in, the £1,000 is £85.

Sit with that one. Nvidia, the company everyone is chasing today, was the best stock in the index in 2001. Buy it the January after, and you lose 83%. Being right about the company is not the same as being right about the price.

Then a few decent years. Avaya, Autodesk, Valero, Allegheny Technologies. Up a bit, then flat. Then 2007's winner, National Oilwell Varco, bought in January 2008, lost 67% in the crash. Then Netflix, the best stock of 2010, lost 61% in 2011.

By the end of 2011, the hype pot's £1,000 is £30. Down 97%. 12 years of buying the stock everyone was talking about.

The boring pot? About £1,280. It had been through the dot-com crash and the 2008 crash and it was still standing. Not exciting. Standing.

**The comeback**

Then the story turns, and this is the part the hype merchants will quote at you.

From 2017 the hype pot gets lucky. Nvidia, the best stock of 2016, goes up another 81%. AMD wins two years running and the hype pot rides it up 148%, then 100%. Tesla, Devon Energy. Then Nvidia again, up 171% in 2024. Then Palantir, up 135% last year.

Nine years of being right, almost in a row. Here is the hype pot at the end of 2025, after sitting at £30 in 2011: about £7,200. The boring pot: about £9,000. Nine straight years of picking the winner's follow-up, and it still has not caught up.

And look at what you had to sit through to get there. This is Nvidia's chart since 2018. The hype pot owned this stock in 2002, when it fell 83%, and sold it at the bottom, because the rules said buy the new winner. The hype pot never owned it on the way up until 2017.

And this year? Last year's winner was Sandisk, and it is up nearly 400% so far. So right now, in October 2026, the hype pot is ahead for the first time in 26 years.

That is the trap. It always looks like it is about to work. But to be here, you had to watch £1,000 become £30, hold a stock that lost 83%, sell it, and never once flinch for a decade. Nobody does that.

Most people who chase winners arrive in year 25, buy at the top, and get the next 2002.

And this is what the boring pot looks like from the inside. An S&P 500 index fund, bought in 2020 and left alone, up about 130% since the start. Nothing clever happened here. Nobody touched it.

**The number that settles it**

And the number I promised you at the start.

A professor called Hendrik Bessembinder studied every US stock since 1926. 26,000 companies. 4% of them, about 1,090, made all of the market's gains. The other 96%, put together, did no better than cash. Most individual stocks lose money over their life.

You cannot guess which 4%. Nobody can. That is what the 15 zeros were telling you.

But an index fund owns all of them, automatically, and it bought Nvidia in 2001 and never sold.

So here is the boring way. One index fund, global or S&P 500. The same amount every month, inside an ISA so the growth is tax-free. Dividends reinvested, nothing sold. And the hype test: if you heard about a stock from a headline, the price already knows.

None of this is advice, and the past is not a promise about the future. It is just 26 years of maths, and the maths does not care how exciting the stock is.

**Recap**

The hype pot: a decade at £30 for £7,200 today. The boring pot: £9,000, and you never lost sleep. 4% of companies make all the gains, and the index owns every one of them.
