**Hook**

If you had bought last year's hottest stock every single January since 2000, your thousand pounds would have been worth about thirty pounds by 2011.

The same thousand pounds in the S&P 500, left completely alone, is about nine thousand pounds today. It never once dropped below seven hundred and fifty.

So I ran the whole race, year by year. I'll show you every year's winner and what it did next, the one year chasing paid off spectacularly, and at the end, the number from the biggest study ever done on this. It settles the argument.

**The rules of the race**

First, the rules, because the rules are the whole point.

Pot one is the boring pot. A thousand pounds into an S&P 500 index fund in January 2000, dividends reinvested, never touched again.

Pot two is the chaser. Every January it buys the stock that was the best performer in the S&P 500 the year before,

the one in every headline, and holds it for twelve months. Then it sells and buys the new winner.

Why last year's winner and not this year's? Because nobody knows this year's winner in January. If you somehow could, your thousand pounds would be a number with fifteen zeros on it, more than all the money on Earth. That is the maths telling you it cannot be done.

**The lost decade**

Now the race. And the first twelve years are brutal.

In 2000 the hottest stock was an energy trader called Dynegy, up more than two hundred percent. The chaser bought it in January 2001, and it fell fifty-four percent. In 2001 the winner was Nvidia, up three hundred and eight percent. The chaser bought that, and in 2002 it lost eighty-three percent. Two years in, the thousand pounds is eighty-five pounds.

Sit with that one. Nvidia, the company everyone is chasing today, was the best stock in the index in 2001. Buy it the January after, and you lose eighty-three percent. Being right about the company is not the same as being right about the price.

Then a few decent years. Avaya, Autodesk, Valero, Allegheny Technologies. Up a bit, then flat. Then 2007's winner, National Oilwell Varco, bought in January 2008, lost sixty-seven percent in the crash. Then Netflix, the best stock of 2010, lost sixty-one percent in 2011.

By the end of 2011, the chaser's thousand pounds is thirty pounds. Down ninety-seven percent. Twelve years of buying the stock everyone was talking about.

The boring pot? About twelve hundred and eighty. It had been through the dot-com crash and the 2008 crash and it was still standing. Not exciting. Standing.

**The comeback**

Then the story turns, and this is the part the hype merchants will quote at you.

From 2017 the chaser gets lucky. Nvidia, the best stock of 2016, goes up another eighty-one percent. AMD wins two years running and the chaser rides it up a hundred and forty-eight percent, then a hundred. Tesla, Devon Energy. Then Nvidia again, up a hundred and seventy-one percent in 2024. Then Palantir, up a hundred and thirty-five percent last year.

Nine years of being right, almost in a row. And here is where the chaser's pot ends 2025: about seven thousand two hundred pounds. The boring pot: about nine thousand. Nine straight years of picking the winner's follow-up, and it still has not caught up.

And look at what you had to sit through to get there. This is Nvidia's chart since 2018. The chaser owned this stock in 2002, when it fell eighty-three percent, and sold it at the bottom, because the rules said buy the new winner. The chaser never owned it on the way up until 2017.

And this year? Last year's winner was Sandisk, and it is up nearly four hundred percent so far. So right now, in October 2026, the chaser's pot is ahead for the first time in twenty-six years.

That is the trap. It always looks like it is about to work. But to be here, you had to watch a thousand pounds become thirty, hold a stock that lost eighty-three percent, sell it, and never once flinch for a decade. Nobody does that.

Most people who chase winners arrive in year twenty-five, buy at the top, and get the next 2002.

And this is what the boring pot looks like from the inside. An S&P 500 index fund, bought in 2020 and left alone, up about a hundred and thirty percent since the start. Nothing clever happened here. Nobody touched it.

**The number that settles it**

And the number I promised you at the start.

A professor called Hendrik Bessembinder studied every US stock since 1926. Twenty-six thousand companies. Four percent of them, about a thousand and ninety, made all of the market's gains. The other ninety-six percent, put together, did no better than cash. Most individual stocks lose money over their life.

You cannot guess which four percent. Nobody can. That is what the fifteen zeros were telling you.

But an index fund owns all of them, automatically, and it bought Nvidia in 2001 and never sold.

So here is the boring way. One index fund, global or S&P 500. The same amount every month, inside an ISA so the growth is tax-free. Dividends reinvested, nothing sold. And the hype test: if you heard about a stock from a headline, the price already knows.

None of this is advice, and the past is not a promise about the future. It is just twenty-six years of maths, and the maths does not care how exciting the stock is.

**Recap**

Chase the winner every year, and you spend a decade at thirty pounds for seven thousand today. Leave it in the index, and it is nine thousand and you never lost sleep.
