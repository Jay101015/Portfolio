[takeaway title="The rules of the race" n="1" of="4"]
First, the rules, because the rules are the whole point.

[text board="Pot one: £1,000 in the index, never touched" anim=hourglass art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:bought January 2000@January; green:dividends reinvested@reinvested"]
Pot one is the boring pot. A thousand pounds into an S&P 500 index fund in January 2000, dividends reinvested, never touched again.

[text board="Pot two: £1,000 that buys last year's winner, every January" anim=phone-ping art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:last year's best stock@best; ink:held 12 months@months"]
Pot two is the chaser. Every January it buys the stock that was the best performer in the S&P 500 the year before, the one in every headline, and holds it for twelve months. Then it sells and buys the new winner.

[text board="Pick the winner in advance? £1,000 → a number with 15 zeros" art=maya-facepalm cards="red:impossible@nobody; ink:you only know in December@December"]
Why last year's winner and not this year's? Because nobody knows this year's winner in January. If you somehow could, your thousand pounds would be a number with fifteen zeros on it, more than all the money on Earth. That is the maths telling you it cannot be done.

[takeaway title="The lost decade" n="2" of="4"]
Now the race. And the first twelve years are brutal.

[list title="2000 to 2002: the chaser's first two buys"]
- 2000's winner: Dynegy, up over 200% → 2001: −54%
- 2001's winner: Nvidia, up 308% → 2002: −83%
- The chaser's pot after two years: £71
In 2000 the hottest stock was an energy trader called Dynegy, up more than two hundred percent. The chaser bought it in January 2001, and it fell fifty-four percent. In 2001 the winner was Nvidia, up three hundred and eight percent. The chaser bought that, and in 2002 it lost eighty-three percent. Two years in, the thousand pounds is seventy-one pounds.

[bite value="£1,000" lose="£830" label="Nvidia, the year after it was the best stock in America" sub="gone in twelve months" source="2002, split-adjusted price"]
Sit with that one. Nvidia, the company everyone is chasing today, was the best stock in the index in 2001. Buy it the January after, and you lose eighty-three percent. Being right about the company is not the same as being right about the price.

[list title="2003 to 2011: eight more winners"]
- Avaya +428% → +33%
- Autodesk, Valero, Allegheny: small gains, then flat
- National Oilwell Varco +140% → 2008: −67%
- Netflix +219% → 2011: −61%
Then a few decent years. Avaya, Autodesk, Valero, Allegheny Technologies. Up a bit, then flat. Then 2007's winner, National Oilwell Varco, bought in January 2008, lost sixty-seven percent in the crash. Then Netflix, the best stock of 2010, lost sixty-one percent in 2011.

[stat value="£25" label="The chaser's pot, end of 2011" sub="down 97.5%" source="worked from each winner's next-year return" anim=money-flies art_at=r art_size=0.5]
By the end of 2011, the chaser's thousand pounds is twenty-five pounds. Down ninety-seven and a half percent. Twelve years of buying the stock everyone was talking about.

[stat value="£1,070" label="The boring pot, end of 2011" sub="through the dot-com crash and 2008" source="Slickcharts, S&P 500 total return 2000 to 2011" anim=hourglass art_at=r art_size=0.5]
The boring pot? About a thousand and seventy. It had been through the dot-com crash and the 2008 crash and it was still standing. Not exciting. Standing.

[takeaway title="The comeback" n="3" of="4"]
Then the story turns, and this is the part the hype merchants will quote at you.

[list title="2012 to 2025: the chaser's run"]
- Nvidia (2016's winner) → 2017: +81%
- AMD (2018 and 2019's winner) → +148%, then +100%
- Tesla → +50%, Devon Energy → +40%
- Nvidia (2023's winner) → 2024: +171%
- Palantir (2024's winner) → 2025: +135%
From 2017 the chaser gets lucky. Nvidia, the best stock of 2016, goes up another eighty-one percent. AMD wins two years running and the chaser rides it up a hundred and forty-eight percent, then a hundred. Tesla, Devon Energy. Then Nvidia again, up a hundred and seventy-one percent in 2024. Then Palantir, up a hundred and thirty-five percent last year.

Nine years of being right, almost in a row. And here is where the chaser's pot ends 2025: about seven thousand two hundred pounds. The boring pot: about nine thousand. Nine straight years of picking the winner's follow-up, and it still has not caught up.

[broll lib="nvda-max-chart-app" phone=on caption="Nvidia, since 2018, in my app" marks="green:circle 50,30@eighty-three"]
And look at what you had to sit through to get there. This is Nvidia's chart in my own app. The chaser owned this stock in 2002, when it fell eighty-three percent, and sold it at the bottom, because the rules said buy the new winner. The chaser never owned it on the way up until 2017.

[text board="2026: the chaser is finally ahead. That's the trap." anim=scales art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:Sandisk +385% this year@Sandisk; red:26 years to get here@twenty-six; ink:nobody holds £25 for a decade@decade"]
And this year? Last year's winner was Sandisk, and it is up nearly four hundred percent so far. So right now, in October 2026, the chaser's pot is ahead for the first time in twenty-six years. That is the trap. It always looks like it is about to work. But to be here, you had to watch a thousand pounds become twenty-five, hold a stock that lost eighty-three percent, sell it, and never once flinch for a decade. Nobody does that. Most people who chase winners arrive in year twenty-five, buy at the top, and get the next 2002.

[takeaway title="The number that settles it" n="4" of="4"]
And the number I promised you at the start.

[stat value="4%" label="of companies made all of the stock market's gains" sub="1,092 out of about 26,000, since 1926" source="Bessembinder, Journal of Financial Economics, 2018" anim=stack-grow art_at=r art_size=0.5]
A professor called Hendrik Bessembinder studied every US stock since 1926. Twenty-six thousand companies. Four percent of them, about a thousand and ninety, made all of the market's gains. The other ninety-six percent, put together, did no better than cash. Most individual stocks lose money over their life.

[text board="You can't guess the 4%. The index owns all of them." art=index-fund-basket cards="green:every winner, automatically@automatically; ink:no guessing@guess"]
You cannot guess which four percent. Nobody can. That is what the fifteen zeros were telling you. But an index fund owns all of them, automatically, and it bought Nvidia in 2001 and never sold.

[list title="The boring way, in four lines"]
- A global or S&P 500 index fund
- The same amount every month, inside an ISA
- Dividends reinvested, nothing sold
- If you heard about it from a headline, it's already in the price
So here is the boring way. One index fund, global or S&P 500. The same amount every month, inside an ISA so the growth is tax-free. Dividends reinvested, nothing sold. And the hype test: if you heard about a stock from a headline, the price already knows.

[text board="Not advice. Just the maths." cards="ink:your money, your call@call"]
None of this is advice, and the past is not a promise about the future. It is just twenty-six years of maths, and the maths does not care how exciting the stock is.
