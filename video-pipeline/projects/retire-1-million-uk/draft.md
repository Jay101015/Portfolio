[broll search="retired couple walking on british seaside promenade" caption="Three ways to retire" cards="ink:minimum, moderate, comfortable@minimum; ink:after tax, outside London@outside" src="06-retired-couple-walking-on-british-seasid.mp4" credit="Video 152798 by ghasoub via Pixabay"]
Every year, pensions UK, the body that represents UK pension schemes, works out what retirement costs at three levels, minimum, moderate and comfortable. These are spending budgets after tax for someone living outside London.

[stat value="£13,900" label="A minimum retirement, one person, a year" sub="the basics, a week in the UK, little else" source="Pensions UK Retirement Living Standards, June 2026, outside London" art=small-basket art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:the basics@basics; red:if something breaks, it hurts@hurts"]
The minimum is about £13 ,900 a year for one person. That covers the basics. Food, bills, a week's holiday in the UK. Not much else. If something breaks, it hurts.

[stat value="£32,700" label="A moderate retirement, one person, a year" sub="a holiday abroad, a car, some security" source="Pensions UK Retirement Living Standards, June 2026, outside London" art=coffee-shop art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:a holiday abroad@abroad; ink:running a car@car; ink:eating out now and then@eating; green:no panic when the boiler goes@boiler"]
Moderate is about £32 ,700. That gets you more security. A holiday abroad each year, running a car, eating out now and then, and not panicking when the boiler goes.

[stat value="£45,400" label="A comfortable retirement, one person, a year" sub="regular holidays, help for family, real freedom" source="Pensions UK Retirement Living Standards, June 2026, outside London" art=airport-departures art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:regular holidays abroad@regular; green:helping your family@helping; green:real freedom@freedom"]
And comfortable is about £45 ,400. That's regular holidays abroad, a newer car, helping your kids or grandkids and real freedom with your time.

[broll search="senior woman drinking tea at kitchen window" caption="What the difference feels like" cards="ink:how it feels@feels"]
The difference between those three isn't really holidays and cars. It's how it feels.

[broll search="older woman checking price label in supermarket" caption="Minimum: saying no a lot" cards="ink:checking every price@checking" src="11-older-woman-checking-price-label-in-supe.mp4" credit="Video 1735 by Buvrix27 via Pixabay"]
On the minimum, you're checking every price and saying no a lot.

[broll search="grandmother playing with grandchild in garden" caption="Comfortable: the one who can help" cards="ink:yes to most normal things@yes; green:the one who can help@help"]
On moderate, you can say yes to most normal things. On comfortable, you're the one who can help when someone in the family needs it.

[list title="For a couple, combined" art=retired-bench art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:cheaper per person@cheaper; green:you share the bills@share; green:£62,700 comfortable@£62,700"]
- Minimum: £22,500
- Moderate: £45,400
- Comfortable: £62,700
If you're a couple, it's cheaper per person because you share the bills. Minimum is about £22 ,500 between you. Moderate, about £45 ,400 and comfortable, about £62 ,700.

[broll lib="tower-bridge-office-window" caption="London costs more" cards="red:London has its own, higher figures@higher; red:rent and travel cost more@travel"]
And if you'll retire in London, every one of those numbers is higher. Pensions UK publishes separate London figures because rent, travel and everything else costs more.

[broll search="hand counting british pound notes on table" caption="Everything in today's money" cards="ink:today's money@today's; ink:allowing for inflation@inflation; ink:otherwise the numbers lie@lie"]
One important thing, all of these are in today's money. So when we work out what you need, we'll keep everything in today's money too and allow for inflation along the way. Otherwise, the numbers lie to you.

[stat value="£0" count=off label="What these budgets include for rent or a mortgage" sub="they assume you own your home outright" source="Pensions UK Retirement Living Standards: the detail" anim=house-build art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:no rent, no mortgage@rent; red:renting needs far more@renting; ink:the most important number in the video@important"]
And here's the part almost nobody mentions. All of these budgets assume you own your home outright by the time you retire. There's nothing in them for rent or a mortgage. So if you're renting when you retire, you need a lot more. We'll come back to exactly how much because for a lot of people watching this, it's the most important number in the video.

[takeaway title="What the state pension covers" n="2" of="6"]
Now, the good news first, you don't have to fund all of it yourself.

[stat value="£241.30" label="The full new state pension, a week, from April 2026" sub="about £12,548 a year" source="DWP benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027" art=pension-letter art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:a week@week; green:£12,548 a year@£12,548; green:it rises every year: the triple lock@triple"]
The full new state pension is £241 .30 a week from April 2026. That's about £12 ,548 a year and it rises every year under the triple lock.

[pie value="90%" label="Of a minimum retirement, covered by the state pension" sub="£12,548 of £13,900" source="Pensions UK, June 2026; DWP rates 2026 to 2027" cards="green:almost all of the minimum@minimum; ink:the floor most people will have@floor"]
So on its own, the full state pension covers about 90 % of a minimum retirement for one person. That's the floor most people will have.

[text board="35 years of National Insurance for the full amount | Fewer than 10 years: nothing" art=brown-envelope art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:35 qualifying years@qualifying; green:caring for children can count@caring"]
But there's a catch. To get the full amount, you need 35 qualifying years of national insurance. With fewer, you get less. With fewer than 10, you get nothing at all. Years caring for children or on certain benefits can count too.

[broll search="hands typing on laptop at kitchen table" caption="Check yours: it takes 5 minutes" cards="green:gov.uk/check-state-pension@check; green:gaps you can fill@gaps" src="21-hands-typing-on-laptop-at-kitchen-table.mp4" credit="Video 10822 by Coverr-Free-Footage via Pixabay"]
You can check exactly what you're on track for on the government's website. Search check state pension on gov .uk. It takes about five minutes and it tells you if you've got gaps you can fill.

[broll search="pensioners walking on british high street" caption="State pension age: 67 by 2028" cards="ink:66 now@66; red:67 by 2028@67; red:set to rise again@again; ink:plan on 67 at the earliest@earliest"]
And you won't get it as early as your parents did. The state pension age is rising from 66 to 67 between 2026 and 2028 and it's set to rise again later. For most people watching this, plan on 67 at the earliest.

[compare title="Two kinds of workplace pension" cards="ink:two kinds of pension@final; ink:a guaranteed income for life@guaranteed; ink:most people under 40: a pot@under; ink:what goes in, how it grows@grows" good=both]
left: Final salary | Promised | A guaranteed income for life
right: A pot | It depends | On what goes in and how it grows
One more thing before the pot. If you have a final salary pension, the kind that pays a guaranteed income for life, your maths is different and probably better because part of your retirement is already promised. But most people under 40 in private sector jobs don't. They have a pot that they and their employer pay into and what it's worth at the end depends on what goes in and how it grows. That's the kind we're working out today.

[takeaway title="The pot you need" n="3" of="6"]
So here's the gap your own pension has to fill and what it means as a pot of money.

[list title="The gap, every year, for one person" art=staircase-coins art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:taxed like a salary@taxed; ink:add the tax, take off the state pension@add"]
- Minimum: almost nothing
- Moderate: about £25,000 a year
- Comfortable: about £42,000 a year
Pension income is taxed like a salary, so you need a bit more than the spending figure. Once you add the tax and take off the state pension, here's the gap. For a minimum retirement, almost nothing. For moderate, about £25 ,000 a year from your own pension. For comfortable, about £42 ,000 a year.

[text board="Pension income is taxed like a salary" art=tax-return art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:£12,570 tax-free@£12,570; ink:the state pension uses most of it@most; red:20% after that@20%; ink:so the pot has to be bigger@bigger"]
Quickly, on that tax. In retirement, you still get your £12 ,570 tax -free personal allowance, and the state pension uses most of it up. So almost every pound your own pension pays you on top is taxed at 20%, or more if your income is high. That's why the pot has to be bigger than the spending gap alone.

[stat value="4%" count=off label="Take out about 4% of your pot a year" sub="to last about thirty years" source="A rule of thumb; some UK research suggests 3.5%" anim=hourglass art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:rising with inflation@rising; ink:thirty years@thirty; ink:a rough guide, not a guarantee@rough; ink:3.5% may be safer@3.5%"]
So how bigger pot pays that? A common rule of thumb is that you can take out about 4 % of your pot each year, rising with inflation and have a good chance of it lasting 30 years. It's a rough guide, not a guarantee. Some UK research says 3 .5 % is safer, but it gives us a number.

[broll search="elderly woman celebrating birthday with family" caption="Plan for your nineties" cards="red:the risk: running out@running; ink:into their nineties@nineties"]
And why 30 years? Because the real risk in retirement isn't dying with money left over, it's running out while you're still here. Plenty of people retiring at 67 today will live into their 90s, so the pot has to last.

[stat value="£630,000" label="The pot for a moderate retirement, one person" sub="in today's money, at the 4% rule" source="Worked out: Pensions UK 2026; 2026-27 tax" art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:on your own@own" anim=stack-grow]
For a moderate retirement on your own, that's a pot of about £630 ,000 in today's money.

[broll search="retired couple looking at laptop together at home" caption="A couple shares the gap" cards="green:about £317,000 each@£317,000; ink:around £634,000 between you@£634,000" src="30-retired-couple-looking-at-laptop-togethe.mp4" credit="Video 6096 by 3593622 via Pixabay"]
For a couple wanting moderate, it's far less each because you have two state pensions and share the bills. About £317 ,000 each, or around £634 ,000 between you.

[broll lib="uk-flats-block-drone" caption="If you're still renting at 67" cards="red:£1,406 a month@£1,406"]
Now remember the rent. Take that moderate retirement on your own and add the average UK private rent of about £1 ,406 a month.

[stat value="£1.23 million" count=off label="The pot for a moderate retirement if you rent" sub="one person, paying the average UK rent" source="Worked out from Pensions UK, June 2026; ONS private rents, October 2026; 2026-27 tax rates" anim=rent-day art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:more than a comfortable homeowner@more"]
Suddenly, the pot you'd need is about £1 .23 million. That's more than a comfortable retirement for a homeowner.

[text board="Paying off your home is a pension decision" art=house-scales art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:one of the biggest decisions you'll make@biggest; ink:it doesn't look like one@look"]
Which is why, for a lot of people, paying off a home before you retire is one of the biggest pension decisions you'll ever make, even though it doesn't look like one.

[chart bar title="On track for each retirement" y="%" source="Pensions UK Retirement Living Standards, June 2026" cards="ink:you're not alone@alone; red:fewer than 1 in 10@fewer; ink:know the number@know"]
bars: Minimum:82, Moderate:23, Comfortable:9
And if those numbers make your stomach drop, you're not alone. In its update this year, pensions UK worked out how many working people are on track. About 82 % are heading for at least the minimum. But only 23 % are on track for moderate and just 9 % for comfortable. That's fewer than one in 10. The point isn't to panic. It's to know the number so you can do something about it.

So, what does it take? Let's work it out for you by the age you start. And stick with me, because after this I'll show you the three leavers that make it far cheaper than it looks. And then, the honest answer to the million pound question.

[text board="Growth: 4% a year, in today's money | Retire at 67" art=payslip art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:everything in today's money@today's"]
Here are the assumptions so you can see I'm not cheating. Your money grows by 4 % a year above inflation. That's after inflation, so everything stays in today's money. And you retire at 67.

[list title="To reach £630,000 by 67, a month:" art=laptop-standing-order art_at=r art_size=0.45 cards="ink:a moderate retirement@moderate"]
- Start at 25: about £483 a month
- Start at 35: about £811 a month
- Start at 45: about £1,492 a month
For a moderate retirement, to reach 630 ,000 pounds by 67, start at 25 and it's about 483 pounds a month. Start at 35, it's about 811 pounds. Start at 45, it's about 1 ,492 pounds a month.

[stat value="3x" count=off label="The cost of starting at 45 instead of 25" sub="£1,492 a month against £483" source="Illustration: 4% a year after inflation; not a promise" anim=scales art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="red:£1,492 vs £483@Starting; red:three times as much@three; ink:the whole video in one number@whole; green:time: the one thing you can't buy back@buy"]
Starting 20 years later doesn't cost you a bit more. It costs you about three times as much, every single month. That's the whole video in one number. Time is the most valuable thing you have, and it's the one thing you can't buy back.

[broll search="middle aged woman working on laptop in office" caption="45 and behind? It's not too late" cards="green:not too late@late; green:your peak earning years@peak" src="39-middle-aged-woman-working-on-laptop-in-o.mp4" credit="Video 27092 by chuduyen215 via Pixabay"]
And if you're 45 and reading those numbers with a sinking feeling, it's not too late. Your 40s and 50s are usually your peak earning years, so you can often put in more.

[broll search="older man sanding wood carpentry workshop" caption="A couple of years longer" cards="green:higher-rate: bigger relief@higher-rate; ink:a couple of years longer@couple; ink:or part-time@part-time"]
If you pay higher rate tax, the tax relief is bigger. And working even a couple of years longer, or going part -time instead of stopping, makes a big difference, because the pot grows for longer and has fewer years to pay for.

[broll search="colleagues working at desks in modern office" caption="£483 a month is not just you" cards="green:your employer pays some@employer; green:the taxman pays some@taxman" src="41-colleagues-working-at-desks-in-modern-of.mp4" credit="Video 7269 by Coverr-Free-Footage via Pixabay"]
And before 483 pounds a month sounds impossible, that's not all coming out of your pay. Your employer and the tax man put in a big chunk of it. Which brings us to the levers.

[takeaway title="The three levers that make it cheaper" n="5" of="6"]
There are three things that make a pension far cheaper than it looks.

[stat value="3%" count=off label="The least your employer must add to your workplace pension" sub="of your qualifying pay" source="GOV.UK workplace pensions, automatic enrolment" art=pension-match art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:lever one: your employer@employer; green:at least 3% from your employer@least; green:some match what you add@match; red:pay left on the table@table"]
Leave a one, your employer. By law, if you're enrolled in a workplace pension, your employer has to put in at least 3 % of your qualifying pay, as long as you put in your share. Plenty put in more, and some will match whatever you add up to a limit. If you're not taking the full match, you're leaving part of your pay on the table.

[pie value="20%" label="Of every £100 you put in, paid by tax relief" sub="a basic-rate taxpayer puts in £80" source="GOV.UK tax on your private pension contributions" cards="ink:lever two: tax relief@relief; green:£80 from you@£80; green:£20 you'd have paid anyway@£20; green:higher rate: as little as £60@£60"]
Leave a two, tax relief. Money you put into a pension comes out before income tax. So for a basic rate tax payer, 100 pounds in your pension only costs you 80 pounds. The other 20 pounds is tax you would have paid anyway. For a higher rate tax payer, that same 100 pounds can cost as little as 60 pounds.

[pie value="25%" label="Of your pot, usually tax-free when you take it" sub="so less to pay than our numbers assumed" source="GOV.UK, tax when you get a pension" cards="green:a quarter tax-free@quarter; green:works in your favour@favour"]
And when you retire, you can usually take up to a quarter of your pot tax -free. Which means the tax on your retirement income is lower than our numbers assumed. That works in your favor.

[text board="Under 40? A Lifetime ISA adds 25%" anim=coins-jar art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:25% on top@25%; ink:up to £4,000 a year@£4,000; green:up to £1,000 a year free@free; ink:locked until 60@60; red:a penalty if you take it early@penalty"]
If you're under 40, there's one more option. A lifetime ISA adds a 25 % government bonus on up to 4 ,000 pounds a year. So up to 1 ,000 pounds a year, free. You can use it for a first home or keep it for retirement. But for retirement, it's locked until 60. And taking it out early for anything else costs you a penalty. Read the rules first.

[text board="Every pay rise: 1% more into your pension" art=pay-rise-ask art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="green:+1%@1%; ink:before you get used to it@used"]
And here's a simple habit that makes all of this painless. Every time you get a pay rise, put 1 % more into your pension before you get used to the money.

[broll search="person opening letter at kitchen table smiling" caption="You'll barely notice it" cards="green:a very different retirement@different" src="48-man-smiling-checking-banking-app-on-phon.mp4" credit="Video 3135 by Life-Of-Vids via Pixabay"]
You'll barely notice it in your take home, and over a career, it adds up to a very different retirement.

[stat value="£540,000" count=off label="£300 a month for 35 years, against 25 years" sub="£243,000 if you start ten years later" source="Illustration: 7% a year average growth before inflation, monthly; not a promise" art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:lever three: time@time; ink:£300 a month@£300; ink:7% a year@7%; green:about £540,000@£540,000; ink:about £243,000@£243,000" anim=plant-grow]
Lever three is time, and it's the biggest. Look at the same 300 pounds a month growing at 7 % a year before inflation. Over 35 years, it grows to about 540 ,000 pounds. Over 25 years, about 243 ,000 pounds. 10 fewer years, and you end up with less than half.

[stat value="£43,700" count=off label="What 1% fees cost on £300 a month over 30 years" sub="against 0.3%, at 7% growth" source="Illustration: 7% a year before fees, monthly; not a promise" art=fee-slice art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:£300 a month@£300; red:1% a year@1%; green:0.3% a year@0.3%; ink:same money in, same markets@Same"]
And there's a quiet fourth lever, fees. On 300 pounds a month, over 30 years, the difference between paying 1 % a year in fees and paying 0 .3 % is about 43 ,700 pounds. Same money in, same markets, just less going to someone else.

[stat value="£31.1 billion" count=off label="In about 3.3 million lost pension pots" sub="old jobs, old addresses, forgotten pots" source="Pensions Policy Institute, Briefing Note 138, October 2024" art=brown-envelope art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:Pensions Policy Institute, 2024@2024; ink:3.3 million lost pots@3.3"]
And while you're checking, in 2024, the Pensions Policy Institute estimated there were about 3 .3 million lost pension pots in the UK, holding about 31 .1 billion pounds.

[broll search="person searching on laptop at kitchen table" caption="The Pension Tracing Service: free" cards="green:the Pension Tracing Service@Tracing; green:some of it might be yours@yours" src="52-person-searching-on-laptop-at-kitchen-ta.mp4" credit="Video 92480 by jamescheney82 via Pixabay"]
Old jobs, old addresses, forgotten pots. If you've changed jobs a few times, search for the government's pension tracing service. It's free, and some of that money might be yours.

[broll lib="vuag-holdings-app" phone=on caption="An index fund, left alone" marks="ink:circle 27,37@hundreds" cards="ink:hundreds of companies@hundreds; ink:no stock picking@picking; green:just years@years"]

[broll search="person logging into app on smartphone at home" caption="A five-minute job this week" cards="ink:log in and look@Log; ink:how much is going in@going; ink:what it's invested in@invested; red:the fees@fees; green:the ones who look put in more@tend" src="54-person-logging-into-app-on-smartphone-at.mp4" credit="Video 130213 by Amorn_mimi via Pixabay"]
So, here's a 5 -minute job for this week. Log in to your workplace pension. Check how much is going in, from you and from your employer, what it's invested in, and what the fees are. Most people have never looked.

[takeaway title="So, do you need £1 million?" n="6" of="6"]
The ones who look tend to put in more. So, back to the question in the title. Do you really need a million pounds to retire?

[stat value="£1.05 million" label="The pot for a comfortable retirement, one person" sub="in today's money, at the 4% rule" source="Worked out: Pensions UK 2026; 2026-27 tax" art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:on your own: about £1.05 million@own; ink:yes, roughly@roughly; green:a couple: about £587,000 each@couple" anim=bar-race]
For a comfortable retirement on your own? Yes, roughly. You'd need a pot of about 1 .05 million pounds in today's money. But for a couple, it's about 587 ,000 pounds each, because you have two state pensions and share the costs.

[text board="What you'd spend a year | Take off the state pension, £12,548 | Times 25" art=calculator-table art_at=r art_size=0.5 cards="ink:what you'd spend, minus £12,548@Take; green:what's left × 25@multiply; ink:the 4% rule turned around@turned; ink:add a bit for tax@tax; red:still renting? Rent goes in@renting"]
And here's how to work out your own number in about 30 seconds. Write down what you'd want to spend a year in retirement, in today's money. Take off the state pension, about 12 ,548 pounds. Then multiply what's left by 25. That's the 4 % rule turned around, and it gives you a rough target pot. Add a bit for tax, and remember that if you'll still be renting, rent goes in the spending.

[compare title="Taking the money out" good=both cards="ink:an annuity: guaranteed for life@annuity; ink:drawdown: stay invested@drawdown; ink:many mix the two@mixing"]

And when you get there, there are two main ways to turn a pot into an income. You can buy an annuity, which pays you a guaranteed amount for the rest of your life. Or you can use drawdown, where the money stays invested and you take out what you need. Many people end up mixing the two.
