# Jurassic Park (1993): why it still looks better than movies made last year

Welcome back. Today we are doing the big one. {point} Nineteen ninety three. Steven Spielberg. A theme park full of dinosaurs. {smug} And a movie that, thirty years later, still looks better than things made last summer.

[poster src="poster.png" title="Jurassic Park" year="1993" director="Steven Spielberg" runtime="127 min" genre="Adventure" starring="Sam Neill, Laura Dern, Jeff Goldblum" tagline="An adventure 65 million years in the making" pose=point]
Jurassic Park. Directed by Steven Spielberg, based on the Michael Crichton novel, one hundred twenty seven minutes, and made for roughly sixty three million dollars.

[still src="gate.png" credit="Illustration" caption="Welcome to Jurassic Park" pose=think]
The setup is simple. A billionaire builds a park, invites a handful of experts to sign off on it, {surprised} and the tour goes about as well as a tour of a dinosaur island can go.

[still src="glass.png" credit="Illustration" caption="The glass of water" pose=idle]
{neutral} Here is the thing I want to talk about first. The glass of water. Before you see a single T. rex, you see ripples in a cup. {point} That is the whole movie in one shot. Spielberg knows that the wait is scarier than the monster.

[list title="Why it still works"]
- The effects are mostly real
- The suspense is built, not bought
- Every character wants something
- John Williams
{point} Four reasons this holds up. One, the effects are mostly real. Two, the suspense is built, not bought. Three, every character wants something. {happy} And four. John Williams.

[still src="trex.png" credit="Illustration" caption="The breakout" pose=shocked]
The T. rex breakout. {shocked} Rain. A dead fence. A goat that is no longer a goat. The animatronic rex was a full size machine that weighed as much as a truck, {laugh} and it reportedly kept malfunctioning in the rain. You can feel the weight of it, because there was weight.

[stat value="15" label="Minutes of dinosaur screen time" sub="In a two hour movie. About six of those are computer generated." source="Widely reported production figures, approximate" pose=crossed]
And here is the number that surprises everyone. {surprised} There are roughly fifteen minutes of dinosaurs in this whole film. About six of those are computer generated. {smug} That restraint is the reason it does not look dated. You never get long enough to see the seams.

[still src="raptor.png" credit="Illustration" caption="The kitchen" pose=think]
{think} The raptors in the kitchen. Two kids, steel counters, and a claw tapping on tile. {shocked} No music for most of it. Just the tapping. It is the best sequence in the movie and there is almost no dinosaur in it.

[compare title="What works and what doesn't"]
left: What doesn't | | The lawyer is a cartoon; The ending is abrupt; Some of the science is nonsense
right: What works | | The pacing; The practical effects; Goldblum, obviously
{angry} Now, what does not work. The lawyer is written like a cartoon. The ending arrives in a hurry. {facepalm} And the science, as every palaeontologist will tell you at a party, is nonsense. {happy} But against that. The pacing is perfect. The practical effects still land. {laugh} And Jeff Goldblum, obviously.

[quote by="Dr. Ian Malcolm"]
> Your scientists were so preoccupied with whether or not they could, they didn't stop to think if they should.
{point} There is one line that gets quoted more than any other, and it is because it is the whole film. Your scientists were so preoccupied with whether or not they could, {smug} they didn't stop to think if they should.

[score value="9" max="10" label="The verdict" verdict="The blueprint for every blockbuster since. Most of them forgot the glass of water." pose=thumbsup]
{think} My score. {thumbsup} Nine out of ten. It is the blueprint for every blockbuster since, and most of them forgot the glass of water.
