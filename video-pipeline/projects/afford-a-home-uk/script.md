[hook board="London's average home needs £111K a year" art="house-price-tag"]
To buy the average home in London, you'd need to earn around £111,000 a year.

[title kicker="The Everyday Investor" subtitle="What £25K, £50K, £100K and £250K buy"]
So what can you actually afford on your salary? In this video, I'll show you what you can buy on £25,000, £50,000, £100,000 and even £250,000, in London and across the UK.

[text board="I'm house hunting too" art="estate-agent-window"]
And then I'll show you exactly where I land, because I'm also house hunting right now.

[text board="Banks lend about 4.5 times your salary" art="mortgage-offer-letter"]
First, the rule that decides almost everything. Most high street lenders will let a first-time buyer borrow around 4 to 4.5 times their income. Buying with a partner, they usually add both incomes together.

[list title="The numbers I'm using"]
- A 10% deposit
- Borrowing 4.5 times salary
- A two-year fix just under 5%
- A 30-year mortgage
So your budget is simple: what the bank will lend you, plus your deposit. In this video, I'll assume a 10% deposit and borrowing of 4.5 times your salary, which is towards the top end of what most lenders will give. And to work out the monthly payments, I'm using today's average two-year fixed rate of just under 5%, over a 30-year mortgage.

[compare title="Where you buy matters most" source="HM Land Registry, 2026"]
left: London | £550K | The average home
right: The UK | £273K | The average home
Now the prices. The average home in London is around £550,000. Across the whole of the UK, it's about £273,000. Exactly half. So where you buy matters more than almost anything else.

[takeaway title="On £25,000 a year" n="1" of="4"]
Let's start at £25,000 a year. At 4.5 times your salary, a bank might lend you around £112,000. Add a £12,000 deposit and your budget is around £125,000. That's around £600 a month.

[text board="£125K: a small-town flat or a terrace" art="first-flat"]
In London, that's very hard to find. Across the UK, it's a flat in a lot of small towns, or a terraced house in some parts of the North East, Wales and Scotland.

[takeaway title="On £50,000 a year" n="2" of="4"]
£50,000. Borrowing jumps to around £225,000. With a £25,000 deposit, that's a budget of £250,000, around £1,200 a month. That's close to the UK average home.

[broll search="apartment building uk" caption="London: a one-bed in the outer boroughs" pick="2" src="10-apartment-building-uk.mp4" credit="Video 34330 by FilipFilipovic via Pixabay"]
In London, it's still mostly a one-bed flat in outer boroughs, if you can find one.

[takeaway title="On £100,000 a year" n="3" of="4"]
£100,000. Higher rate taxpayer territory. Borrowing reaches around £450,000 with a £50,000 deposit. Your budget is about £500,000, around £2,400 a month.

[text board="Above Essex, still short of London" art="london-essex-map"]
That's well above the average home in Essex. But even then, you're still short of the average home in London.

[takeaway title="On £250,000 a year" n="4" of="4"]
And now, £250,000 a year. Top 1% territory. At 4.5 times, the bank could lend you around £1.125 million. With a £125,000 deposit, that's a £1.25 million home and a mortgage payment of around £6,000 a month.

[text board="No first-time buyer relief up here" art="stamp-duty-receipt"]
At that price, there's no first-time buyer relief on stamp duty, so you pay around £69,000 in stamp duty alone.

[text board="£250K a year: an average home, not a mansion" art="maya-shrug"]
Even a quarter of a million a year salary gets you roughly an average home in Kensington or Chelsea. Not a mansion.

[list title="The average London home"]
- £55,000 saved
- An income of £111,000
- £2,700 a month
So let's talk about London honestly. To buy the average home in London with a 10% deposit, you'd need around £55,000 saved and an income of around £111,000. The monthly payment would be around £2,700.

[stat value="£1.2M" label="Average home in Kensington and Chelsea" sub="it drags the London average up" source="HM Land Registry, August 2026"]
But London isn't one price. That average gets dragged up a lot by places like Kensington and Chelsea, where the average home is over £1.2 million.

[stat value="£376,000" label="The average home in Barking" sub="£37,500 saved, £75K income, £1,800 a month"]
Go further out and it changes a lot. In Barking, the average home is around £376,000. With a 10% deposit, that's about £37,500 saved, an income around £75,000 and a monthly payment of about £1,800.

[stat value="£449,000" label="The average home in Havering" sub="£45,000 saved, £90K income, £2,170 a month"]
In Havering, around the Essex border, it's around £449,000. That needs about £45,000 saved, an income of around £90,000 and roughly £2,170 a month.

[text board="£75K on your own, for a flat" art="sam-worried"]
So if you're searching the outer boroughs, that's your best shot. But you'd still need a salary of around £75,000 or more on your own. And for a house rather than a flat, the numbers get tight really fast.

[broll search="commuter train uk" caption="Looking outside the city" pick="1" src="21-commuter-train-uk.mp4" credit="Video 3608 by Coverr-Free-Footage via Pixabay"]
Which is exactly why so many Londoners look outside the city. And that's where my story comes in.

[stat value="£360,000" label="The average home in Essex" sub="£200K less than London" source="HM Land Registry, 2026"]
The average home in Essex is around £360,000. That's £200,000 less than the average home in London, and many towns are still a short train ride into the city.

[chart bar title="The average home, town by town" y="£" source="HM Land Registry, 2026"]
bars: Harlow and Thurrock:320000, Southend:328000, Basildon:367000, Chelmsford:378000
And it varies a lot by town. Harlow, Thurrock: the average home there is around £320,000. Southend, around £328,000. Basildon, £367,000, and Chelmsford, £378,000.

[broll search="estate agent window property listings" caption="It depends on the street" pick="3" src="24-estate-agent-window-property-listings.mp4" credit="Video 29886 by conolan via Pixabay"]
Those are the averages for all homes, so a two or three bed can sit above or below, depending on the street.

[text board="My search: a 2 to 3 bed in Essex" art="house-viewing"]
So here's what I'm looking for: a two to three bedroom house in Essex.

[list title="My numbers"]
- £60,000 salary
- £270,000 from the bank
- £30-40K deposit
- About £310,000 to spend
On my salary of £60,000, a bank would lend me around £270,000. With my deposit of £30 to £40K, my budget comes to around £310,000. That puts me right in the range for a two or three bedroom house, looking around places such as Tilbury.

[text board="The real wall is the deposit" art="deposit-wall"]
For most people, though, the real wall isn't the mortgage. It's the deposit.

[stat value="£36,000" label="10% down on the average Essex home" sub="3 years at £1,000 a month"]
Take the average Essex home. A 10% deposit is around £36,000. Save £1,000 a month and that takes around three years.

[stat value="+£1,000" label="Lifetime ISA bonus on £4,000 a year" sub="2 years 9 months instead of 3" source="GOV.UK"]
But put £4,000 a year into a Lifetime ISA and the government adds £1,000 per year on top. That cuts it to around two years nine months. Free money, just for saving in the right account.

[text board="£1,000 saved every payday, first" art="laptop-standing-order"]
And that's exactly why I put £1,000 into savings every payday, automatically, before I can even spend it. It's the least glamorous part of buying a home, but it's the most important.

[text board="The costs nobody talks about" art="sam-surprised"]
But the house price isn't the whole story. There are costs nobody talks about.

[list title="First-time buyer stamp duty, England"]
- £0 on the first £300,000
- 5% from £300K to £500K
- Over £500K: no relief
First, stamp duty. As a first-time buyer in England, you pay nothing on the first £300,000, then 5% on the part between £300,000 and £500,000. So on a £360,000 home, that's about £3,000. On £400,000, £5,000. And if the home costs more than £500,000, you lose the first-time buyer relief.

[text board="Solicitors, surveys, fees: a few thousand more" art="moving-boxes"]
Then there are solicitors, surveys, mortgage fees and moving costs. Budget a few thousand pounds on top of your deposit so you're not caught short.

[text board="Three ways to stretch, each with a catch" art="crossroads"]
So what if the numbers don't quite reach? There are a few ways people stretch their budget, and each one has a catch.

[compare title="One: a bigger income multiple"]
left: 4.5 times | £225K | On a £50,000 salary
right: 5.5 times | £275K | On a £50,000 salary; Bigger payments
One: a bigger income multiple. Some lenders go up to about 5.5 times your income, usually for certain professions or higher earners. On £50,000, that's about £275,000 instead of £225,000. The catch: bigger borrowing means bigger payments, and not everyone qualifies.

[compare title="Two: a longer mortgage"]
left: 30 years | £1,740 | A month on £324,000
right: 35 years | £1,635 | A month on £324,000; Five more years of interest
Two: a longer mortgage. On a £324,000 mortgage, going from 30 years to 35 drops the payment from about £1,740 a month to around £1,635. The catch: you pay interest for five more years, so it costs you more in total.

[text board="Three: shared ownership" art="shared-ownership-house"]
Three: shared ownership. You buy a share of the home, often between 25 and 75%, and pay rent on the rest. It's a way in with a smaller deposit. The catch: you're paying rent and a mortgage, and buying more of the home later can be more expensive.

[pie value="50%" label="Half your take-home pay on the mortgage" sub="nothing left for living"]
And here's the big one. Just because a bank will lend you something doesn't mean you should borrow all of it. A mortgage that eats half of your take-home pay leaves nothing for an emergency fund, investing or actually living.

[text board="Pick the payment, then work backwards" art="calculator-table"]
Work out a monthly payment that you're comfortable with and work backwards.

[recap title="The short version"]
- Banks lend about 4.5 times your salary
- The average London home needs £111K
- Budget for stamp duty and fees on top
So, the short version. Banks will usually lend you around 4.5 times your salary. The average London home needs an income of around £111,000. Always budget for stamp duty and fees on top.
