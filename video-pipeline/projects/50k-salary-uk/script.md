[hook board="£50,000 a year: still a good salary?" art=payday art_at=r art_size=0.5]
£50,000 a year. For years, that was the salary that meant you'd made it. So is it still a good one?

[stat value="4 in 5" count=off label="UK taxpayers earn less than £50,000" sub="but it buys far less than it did" source="HMRC Personal Incomes Statistics 2023 to 2024, April 2026" art=staircase-coins art_at=r art_size=0.5]
The short answer is yes, on paper. £50,000 is more than about 4 in 5 UK taxpayers earn. But it buys a lot less than it used to, and by the end of a normal month, less of it is left than you'd think.

[text board="One month on £50,000 | Line by line" art=payslip art_at=r art_size=0.5]
So let's run one month on £50,000, line by line. And at the end, I'll show you the salary you'd need today to live the way £50,000 did in 2010.

[title dur=2 kicker="The Everyday Investor" title="Is £50,000 still a good salary?" subtitle="One month, line by line"]

[receipt title="One month on £50,000" left="Left" board="Meet Sam" art=payday part="The payslip"]
- Pay before tax: £4,167
Meet Sam. He's 29, he lives on his own, and he's just been promoted to £50,000 a year. That's £4,167 a month, before anyone else gets to it.

[receipt board="First, the taxman" source="Income tax and National Insurance rates for 2026-27, GOV.UK"]
- Income tax: -£624
- National Insurance: -£250
And the first one to get to it is the taxman. Income tax takes £624 a month. National Insurance takes another £250. So before Sam has seen a penny, about a fifth of his pay has gone.

[stat value="£3,293" label="What £50,000 lands as, a month" sub="after income tax and National Insurance" source="Worked out at 2026-27 rates; Scotland's bands are different" art=sam-counting art_at=r art_size=0.5]
That leaves £3,293 a month. If you've ever looked up the take-home pay on £50,000, that's the number you'll have seen. For most people, though, it's not the real one.

[receipt board="Then the two most people forget" source="Workplace pension: 5% of qualifying earnings, employer at least 3% (GOV.UK, 2026-27)"]
- Pension: -£146
Sam's in his workplace pension, like most employees, and that takes about £146 off his take-home. That one isn't lost: it's still his money, just later, and his employer adds at least £109 a month on top. But it's not money he can spend this month.

[receipt board="Then the two most people forget" source="Student loan, Plan 2: 9% of pay over £29,385 (GOV.UK, 2026-27)" art=graduate]
- Student loan: -£155
And like most graduates his age, he's repaying a student loan: 9% of everything he earns over £29,385. That's another £155 a month.

[broll search="man checking banking app on phone" caption="£2,992 a month" cards="ink:started at £4,167@£4,167; ink:about £36,000 a year@£36,000"]
So the salary that started the month at £4,167 is now £2,992. That's about £36,000 a year actually reaching his account. And he hasn't paid for a single thing yet.

[receipt board="Day one: the rent" source="ONS Private rent and house prices, October 2026" part="The month"]
- Rent: -£1,140
Now the month starts. The average one-bedroom home in the UK rents for £1,140 a month, and Sam's flat is exactly that. Nothing fancy.

[broll lib="uk-flats-block-drone" caption="38% gone on day one" cards="red:38% of what reaches him@38%"]
That's 38% of everything that reaches him, gone on the first of the month. A common rule of thumb says keep rent to about a third of your pay. Sam is over that, on an average flat, with a salary in the top fifth. It's the biggest line on this receipt by a distance. Remember that, because it matters later.

[receipt board="The bills that don't care what you earn" source="Council Tax levels 2026 to 2027, MHCLG, March 2026; Ofgem price cap, October to December 2026"]
- Council tax: -£150
- Gas and electricity: -£144
Then the bills that don't care what you earn. The average Band D council tax bill in England is £2,392 a year. Sam lives alone, so he gets a quarter off: about £150 a month. Gas and electricity, at the current price cap for a typical home, about £144.

[receipt board="What's left for everything else" note="about £51 a day"]
Which leaves £1,558. About £51 a day for food, travel, his phone, the car, a holiday, and anything he wants to save. That's a good life. Sam is fine. And Sam has it easy: no children, no car on finance, no debts to clear. But it's not the life £50,000 used to mean, and in a minute I'll show you exactly how much has gone missing.

[broll lib="tower-bridge-office-window" caption="London: average rent £2,338" cards="red:78% of his money@78%"]
And that's outside London. Move Sam to London, where the average rent is £2,338 a month, and that one line would eat 78% of his money. Which is why so many people on £50,000 in London share a flat, or live an hour out.

[chart bar title="Average rent a month" y="£" source="ONS Private rent and house prices, October 2026"]
bars: London:2338, Bristol:1893, Manchester:1380, Birmingham:1106, North East:790
And it's not only London. In Bristol, the average rent is £1,893. In Manchester, £1,380. In Birmingham, £1,106. And across the North East of England, it's £790.

[compare title="Same payslip, different city" good=right source="ONS Private rent and house prices, October 2026"]
left: London | £2,338 | Average rent a month
right: North East | £790 | Average rent a month
Same job, same payslip, and about £1,500 a month more in your pocket in the North East than in London. On £50,000, where you live matters more than almost anything you do with the money.

[text board="So where did the reputation come from?" art=crossroads art_at=r art_size=0.5 part="£50,000 in 2010"]
So why does £50,000 still sound like so much? Because it used to be. Let's go back to 2010.

[compare title="£50,000 against typical full-time pay" good=left source="ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings, 2010 and 2026 (provisional)"]
left: 2010 | Nearly double | Typical pay: £25,900
right: 2026 | A fifth more | Typical pay: £40,911
In 2010, the typical full-time salary in the UK was £25,900. So £50,000 was nearly double what most people earned. Today, the typical full-time salary is £40,911. £50,000 is about a fifth more.

[broll search="office workers at desks open plan" caption="Good, not exceptional"]
That's the first thing that changed. £50,000 went from a salary that set you apart, to one that's a bit above the person sitting next to you. And that 4 in 5 from the start counts every taxpayer, part-timers and pensioners included. Among people working full time, £50,000 is good. It's not exceptional.

[compare title="The same £50,000 payslip" good=right source="Worked out at 2010-11 and 2026-27 income tax and National Insurance rates"]
left: 2010 | £2,984 | A month, after tax
right: 2026 | £3,293 | A month, after tax
Now look at the payslip. In 2010, £50,000 put £2,984 a month in your account. Today, it's £3,293. That's £309 a month more. After sixteen years. And that's with National Insurance being cut from 11% to 8% along the way.

[broll search="supermarket checkout scanning groceries" caption="Prices since 2010" cards="red:up 61%@61%"]
And over those sixteen years, prices have gone up by 61%.

[stat value="£4,810" label="The 2010 payslip, in today's money" sub="Sam gets £3,293" source="ONS Consumer Prices Index, September 2026 against the 2010 average" art=inflation-trolley art_at=r art_size=0.5]
To buy what it bought back then, that 2010 payslip would need to be £4,810 a month today. Sam gets £3,293. In real terms, the same salary has taken a pay cut of about £1,500 a month.

[text board="The 40% line in 2010: £43,875 | Today: £50,270 | If it had kept up: about £70,700" art=tax-return art_at=r art_size=0.5]
A big part of that is tax. In 2010, the 40% rate started at £43,875. Today it starts at £50,270. If that line had kept up with prices, it would be at about £70,700.

[broll search="payslip paper on desk with calculator" caption="A tax rise nobody announces"]
Here's how that works. Wages rise with prices, but the tax lines don't. So a pay rise that only keeps you level with prices still pushes more of your pay into a higher band. It's a tax rise nobody has to announce.

[stat value="7.7 million" label="People paying higher-rate income tax this year" sub="about 3 million in 2010" source="HMRC Income Tax liabilities statistics, July 2026 (projection for 2026-27)" art=bank-queue art_at=r art_size=0.5]
In 2010, about 3 million people paid the higher rate. This year, it's 7.7 million. And that line is frozen until 2031.

[broll lib="businessman-phone-city" caption="One pay rise away" cards="red:keeps 58p of every extra £1@58p; red:49p with a student loan@49p"]
Sam is one small pay rise away from joining them. And once he's over that line, he keeps 58p of every extra pound. With his student loan, 49p.

[compare title="The average UK home, in £50,000 salaries" good=left source="UK House Price Index, HM Land Registry, August 2010 and August 2026"]
left: 2010 | 3 times | About £160,000
right: 2026 | 5.5 times | £273,000
And then there's the thing a good salary was supposed to buy. In 2010, the average home in the UK cost about £160,000: just over 3 times a £50,000 salary. Today it's £273,000. That's five and a half times.

[broll lib="uk-semis-street-pan" caption="4.5 times £50,000 is £225,000" cards="ink:£225,000 mortgage@£225,000; red:the average home: £273,000@doesn't"]
A lender will usually go to around four and a half times your salary. On £50,000, that's £225,000. In 2010, that bought the average home with money to spare. Today it doesn't reach it, and that's before you've saved a deposit.

[broll lib="estate-agent-sold" caption="The number stayed the same"]
So the salary stayed the same, the tax line barely moved, and the price of nearly everything it was meant to pay for went up by more than half. That's why £50,000 doesn't feel the way it sounds.

[text board="Three things that make it go further" art=road-to-100k art_at=r art_size=0.5 part="Making it go further"]
So if you're on £50,000, or you're heading there, three things make it go a lot further.

[stat value="£4,700" count=off label="Of a rise from £50,000 to £55,000, the part over the 40% line" sub="as pay, Sam keeps about £2,300 of it" source="2026-27 rates; pension paid by salary sacrifice, other schemes differ a little" art=pension-match art_at=r art_size=0.5]
First, your pension. Say a pay rise takes Sam to £55,000. About £4,700 of that sits above the 40% line. Taken as pay, he keeps about £2,300 of it. Put into his pension instead, the full £4,700 goes into his pot.

[broll search="to let sign outside terraced house" caption="The biggest line on the receipt" cards="green:£200 a month off the rent@£200"]
Second, housing. It's the biggest line on the receipt, so £200 a month off the rent does more than every coffee you'll ever skip.

[stat value="£366,000" count=off label="£300 a month for 30 years, growing at 7% a year" sub="£108,000 paid in" source="Illustration: 7% a year average growth, monthly, before fees; not a promise" art=laptop-standing-order art_at=r art_size=0.5]
And third, decide what the leftover is for before it lands. Move a fixed amount into savings or investments on payday, and live on the rest. Even £300 a month, growing at 7% a year, is about £366,000 after 30 years. That's an illustration, not a promise, and this is education, not advice: your numbers won't be Sam's.

[broll search="couple at kitchen table with laptop and bills" caption="Who's relying on it?"]
For me, whether £50,000 is enough comes down to who's relying on it. On your own, like Sam, it's a good life. With a mortgage to clear and other people depending on you, it's a squeeze.

[text board="What would £50,000 in 2010 be today?" art=sam-thinking art_at=r art_size=0.5 part="The answer"]
So, the number I promised at the start. What would you have to earn today to live the way £50,000 did in 2010?

[stat value="£81,000" label="What it takes today to match £50,000 in 2010" sub="the same take-home, after 61% higher prices" source="Worked out from 2010-11 and 2026-27 tax rates and ONS CPI, September 2026" art=sam-surprised art_at=r art_size=0.5]
About £81,000. That's the salary that gives you the same take-home pay today, once you allow for sixteen years of rising prices.

[stat value="Top 10%" count=off label="starts at £67,400" sub="£81,000 is well inside it" source="HMRC Personal Incomes Statistics 2023 to 2024, April 2026" art=staircase-coins art_at=r art_size=0.5]
And £81,000 isn't just a good salary. The top 10% of UK taxpayers starts at £67,400. So the life that £50,000 bought in 2010 now takes a top 10% income.

[receipt board="Still good. No longer rich."]
So is £50,000 still a good salary? Yes. It's more than 4 in 5 people earn, and on your own, outside London, it's a comfortable life. It's just not a rich one any more. So if you're on £50,000 and it doesn't feel like much, you're not bad with money. The number stayed the same. Everything around it moved.
